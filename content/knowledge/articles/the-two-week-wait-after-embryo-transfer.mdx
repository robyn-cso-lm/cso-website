The transfer is done. The embryo is where it needs to be. And now everyone, the surrogate, the intended parents, the partners, maybe even the grandparents, is staring at the calendar waiting for one blood test.

Welcome to the two-week wait. It is often the longest two weeks of the entire journey, and it is hard for everyone in a different way. I have been walking families and surrogates through this wait since I was 4 years old, watching my mother do it before me, and I want to give you an honest picture of what is happening and how to get through it.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your fertility clinic will give you instructions for your own transfer. Follow their advice over anything you read online, including here.*

## What is actually happening in there

With a frozen embryo transfer, the embryo is usually a blastocyst, an embryo about five or six days old. In the first few days after transfer, it typically "hatches" from its outer shell and starts to attach to the lining of the uterus. If it implants, it begins producing hCG, the pregnancy hormone that tests detect.

That hCG has to build up before a test can pick it up. That is why clinics do not test the day after transfer, and why it is so frustrating to wait.

The "two-week wait" is a nickname. Many clinics schedule the blood test, called a beta hCG, somewhere around 9 to 14 days after transfer. Your clinic will tell you your exact date. You can learn more about what the numbers mean in [beta hCG levels explained](/knowledge-centre/beta-hcg-levels-explained).

## Symptoms, or no symptoms at all

This is the part I wish everyone understood before transfer day: **symptoms during the two-week wait tell you almost nothing.**

The surrogate is taking estrogen and progesterone, often in fairly high doses. Progesterone alone can cause tiredness, bloating, cramping, sore breasts, mood swings and mild spotting. Those are also the classic early pregnancy signs. So a surrogate can feel "pregnant" and not be, or feel completely normal and be very pregnant.

I have seen surrogates who felt nothing at all get wonderful beta numbers. I have seen surrogates who were sure it worked get a negative. Please do not read the tea leaves, and intended parents, please do not ask your surrogate to analyze every twinge.

Call the clinic if the surrogate has heavy bleeding, severe pain, fever, or anything that worries her.

## Should we use home pregnancy tests?

This is a personal choice, and it is a big one to talk about **before** transfer day.

Some reasons people choose to test at home early:

- They would rather know gradually than all at once from a phone call.
- The surrogate wants to prepare herself emotionally.

Some reasons people choose not to:

- Home tests can be negative early even when a pregnancy is starting, which causes needless heartbreak.
- A faint positive can be followed by a negative beta.
- If a trigger shot or other hCG medication was used, a home test can give a false positive. (Many frozen transfer protocols do not use one, but ask your clinic.)

The most important point: **whatever happens with a home test, the surrogate keeps taking every medication until the clinic says otherwise.** A negative home test is not a reason to stop.

## Who finds out first, and how

This is where surrogacy is different from a regular IVF cycle. There are at least two families waiting, and sometimes more. Agree on a plan in advance:

- Will the surrogate test at home? If yes, will she share the results, or keep them to herself until the beta?
- Who does the clinic call with the beta result? The surrogate, the intended parents, or both?
- Do you want to be on a call together when the result comes in?
- If the news is not good, how does everyone want to hear it?

There is no right answer. What matters is that nobody feels blindsided. In my experience, families who talked this through ahead of time handle both good and hard news with so much more grace.

## For the surrogate: getting through it

- **Keep taking your medications** exactly on schedule. Alarms are your best friend.
- **Follow your clinic's activity advice.** Many clinics say normal daily life is fine, with no heavy lifting, hot tubs or intense exercise for a while. Strict bed rest is not usually recommended, but ask your clinic.
- **Stay busy.** Plan movies, walks, crafts, outings with your kids, anything that keeps your mind off symptom checking.
- **Set boundaries kindly.** If you need fewer check-in texts, it is okay to say so. Something like "I promise I will tell you the second I know anything" goes a long way.
- **Let yourself feel it.** Many surrogates feel a lot of pressure during this wait, because they want so badly to give the intended parents good news. Please remember: whether an embryo implants is not something you can control. You are doing your part by showing up and following the plan.

## For intended parents: getting through it

- **Lean on each other, not just on your surrogate.** She is carrying a lot already.
- **Send kindness, not questions.** A meal delivery, a card, a note saying "thank you, no matter what" means more than "how are you feeling today?"
- **Find a distraction you both enjoy.** Plan something for the day of the beta, whatever the result.
- **Get support.** Fertility counselling exists for exactly this kind of waiting. Our article on [infertility grief and mental health](/knowledge-centre/infertility-grief-and-mental-health) has more on caring for yourselves.

## If the beta is positive

Celebrate! Then expect more waiting. The clinic usually repeats the blood test a few days later to see that the hCG is rising, then books an early ultrasound to look for a heartbeat. The surrogate keeps taking her medications until the clinic weans her off. Read [from positive test to OB care](/knowledge-centre/positive-pregnancy-test-to-ob-care-surrogacy) for what comes next.

## If the beta is negative

This is heartbreaking, and I will not pretend otherwise. Give yourselves time to grieve. A negative transfer is unfortunately common and does not mean the next one will fail or that anyone did anything wrong. Your clinic will usually review the cycle and talk about a plan. See [what happens if an embryo transfer fails](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-if-an-embryo-transfer-fails) and [how many embryo transfers it usually takes](/knowledge-centre/faqs/how-many-embryo-transfers-does-it-take).

Please check in on your surrogate too. Many surrogates feel real grief and even guilt after a negative, even though it is never their fault.
