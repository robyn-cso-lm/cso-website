Here is something many people do not realize until late in the pregnancy: after a surrogate gives birth, her body will make milk whether a baby is nursing or not. That leaves everyone with a decision. Will she pump breast milk for the baby, or will she let her milk dry up?

There is no right answer. Some surrogates love pumping and see it as one more gift. Others want their body back and a clean emotional finish. Both choices are beautiful, and both deserve respect. I grew up in this field, and the journeys where this went well were the ones where it was talked about early and without pressure.

*This article is general information, not medical or legal advice. The surrogate's doctor, midwife or a lactation consultant can advise on her health, and your lawyers can explain how your agreement handles breast milk.*

## Why her body makes milk

During pregnancy, hormones prepare the breasts to make milk. After the placenta is delivered, hormone levels shift and milk production starts, usually within the first few days. This happens after every birth, including a surrogacy birth, and including a caesarean birth.

So even if the surrogate never plans to pump, she will need a plan for how to manage that milk coming in.

## Talk about it early

Breast milk is a conversation for matching or early pregnancy, not the delivery room. Questions to talk through together:

- Is the surrogate interested in pumping at all? For how long, roughly?
- Would she pump only in the hospital (to give the baby early milk, called colostrum), or for weeks after?
- How would milk get to the baby, especially if the families live far apart?
- What supplies and costs would be involved, and how would they be reimbursed?
- How will everyone feel if she needs or wants to stop sooner than planned?

The last question matters most. Pumping can be harder than people expect. Supply can be lower than hoped, and life with her own kids, work and recovery is busy. Any plan should make it easy for her to stop without guilt.

Your surrogacy agreement can include what you agree on, and your lawyers will make sure it is written clearly. For more on agreements, see [what is a surrogacy agreement](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-is-a-surrogacy-agreement).

## If the surrogate decides to pump

Here is what a pumping arrangement usually involves.

### In the hospital

Some surrogates give colostrum while still in hospital, either by pumping or by hand expression. Some hospitals have lactation consultants who can help. Whether the surrogate nurses the baby directly is a personal decision for her and the intended parents. Many prefer pumping so the parents can feed the baby themselves.

### After going home

- **Equipment:** a good quality double electric pump, bottles, storage bags, and cleaning supplies
- **A schedule:** to keep supply up, pumping is usually needed several times a day, including at night in the early weeks
- **Storage:** a freezer, and sometimes a chest freezer if she pumps for a while
- **Getting milk to the baby:** pickups, drop-offs or shipping with dry ice or proper coolers. Long-distance shipping takes planning, so ask a lactation consultant about safe handling and storage.

### Health and safety

Surrogates are screened for many infectious diseases before transfer, but breast milk raises its own questions, such as medications she takes after the birth and her diet. The baby's doctor and the surrogate's care provider can help everyone make a safe plan. If the baby is premature or in the NICU, the hospital will have its own rules for bringing in expressed milk.

## How pumping is reimbursed in Canada

Surrogacy in Canada is altruistic. Under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*, surrogates cannot be paid, and that includes not being paid for breast milk. What intended parents can do, according to the agreement, is reimburse **receipted expenses** connected to pumping, such as:

- Pump rental or purchase, and parts
- Storage bags, bottles and cleaning supplies
- Shipping, coolers and dry ice
- Travel or mileage for milk drop-offs
- Lactation consultant visits
- Other documented costs the agreement allows

As with all surrogacy expenses, keep every receipt. Your lawyers will confirm what your agreement covers. See our [surrogacy reimbursements guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-reimbursements-canada-guide) and [what expenses are reimbursed to a surrogate](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-expenses-are-reimbursed-to-a-surrogate).

## If the surrogate decides not to pump

This is a completely valid choice, and intended parents should never pressure a surrogate to change her mind. Formula-fed babies thrive. Donor milk from a milk bank may also be an option in some situations, which you can ask your baby's care team about.

## Lactation suppression: drying up milk

If she is not pumping, or when she is ready to stop, her body needs to slow and stop milk production. This usually takes a while and can be uncomfortable. Things care providers commonly suggest include:

- A firm, supportive bra (but not binding tightly, which can cause problems)
- Cold packs on the breasts for comfort
- Avoiding stimulation and pumping, other than expressing just a little for comfort if she is very full
- Over-the-counter pain relief, if her care provider says it is okay
- When stopping after pumping, cutting back gradually rather than all at once, to lower the chance of blocked ducts

Some care providers may discuss medication to help suppress milk in certain situations. That is a decision for her and her doctor, since medications have side effects and are not right for everyone.

She should call her care provider if she gets a hard, red, painful area in the breast, a fever, or flu-like symptoms, since those can be signs of mastitis.

## The emotional side

Milk coming in can bring up feelings. For some surrogates it is a beautiful reminder of what their body did. For others it is a surprise that makes the postpartum period feel harder. Hormones are shifting fast, and that is normal. Our [postpartum recovery for surrogates](/knowledge-centre/postpartum-recovery-for-surrogates) guide talks more about the fourth trimester.

## What about the intended mother?

Some intended mothers, and some non-gestational parents, want to feed the baby themselves. It is possible for some people to produce milk without being pregnant, with medical support. Read [inducing lactation for intended mothers](/knowledge-centre/inducing-lactation-intended-mothers) to learn more.
