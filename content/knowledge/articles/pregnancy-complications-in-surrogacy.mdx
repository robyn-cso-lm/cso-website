Most surrogacy pregnancies go smoothly. Surrogates are carefully screened, many have had easy pregnancies before, and they get excellent prenatal care. But any pregnancy can have bumps along the way, and both surrogates and intended parents deserve to know what those might look like and what happens if they do.

I am a big believer in radical honesty. I grew up in this field, and I have seen that families and surrogates who talked about complications before transfer handled them far more calmly when they came up. So let us talk about it.

*This article is general information, not medical or legal advice. Your surrogate's doctor or midwife is the right person for medical questions, and your lawyers will explain how your agreement applies.*

## First, who makes medical decisions?

During the pregnancy, the surrogate is the patient. Her doctor or midwife works with her, and decisions about her care are hers to make, guided by medical advice. A good surrogacy agreement sets out how information will be shared with the intended parents and how everyone hopes to approach different situations, but it cannot override the surrogate's right to decide about her own body. You can read more in [what is a surrogacy agreement](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-is-a-surrogacy-agreement).

This is not something to fear. In my experience, surrogates almost always want exactly what the intended parents want: a healthy baby and a healthy surrogate.

## Common complications, in plain words

Here are some of the more common things that can come up. This is not a full list, and how each one is managed depends entirely on the surrogate's care provider.

### Gestational diabetes

Gestational diabetes is high blood sugar that develops during pregnancy. Most pregnant people in Canada are offered a screening test in the middle of pregnancy. If it is diagnosed, it is often managed with diet changes, blood sugar checks and sometimes medication or insulin. It usually goes away after the birth.

**What it means in practice:** extra appointments, maybe a dietitian, glucose testing supplies, and possibly more monitoring of the baby's growth near the end.

### High blood pressure and preeclampsia

Some people develop high blood pressure during pregnancy. Preeclampsia is a more serious condition involving high blood pressure and signs that other organs are affected. It can come on in the second half of pregnancy or even after the birth. Warning signs can include bad headaches, vision changes, sudden swelling of the face or hands, and pain under the ribs. Anyone with these symptoms should get medical care right away.

**What it means in practice:** more frequent checkups and bloodwork, sometimes medication, and in some cases an earlier delivery for the safety of the surrogate and the baby.

### Placenta concerns

Sometimes the placenta sits low near the cervix (placenta previa) or has other issues. Many low-lying placentas move up as the uterus grows. If not, the surrogate may need activity limits and a planned caesarean.

### Bed rest and activity restrictions

Strict bed rest is prescribed less often than it used to be, but a surrogate may be told to reduce activity, stop working early, or stay in hospital for monitoring. This can be a big deal for her household, and it is one of the main reasons agreements plan for lost wages and help at home.

### Preterm labour and preterm birth

A baby born before 37 weeks is considered preterm. Signs of preterm labour can include regular contractions, low back pain, pressure, or fluid leaking. Care providers can sometimes slow labour or give medications that help the baby's lungs. A preterm baby may need time in the NICU, which can mean the intended parents need to stay near the hospital longer than planned.

### Bleeding, illness and other surprises

Spotting, infections, injuries, severe nausea, and other issues can come up in any pregnancy. The rule is the same: the surrogate calls her care provider, follows medical advice, and keeps the intended parents updated as agreed.

## How surrogacy agreements handle complications

A well-written Canadian surrogacy agreement usually plans for complications before they happen. Common sections include:

- **Medical care and information sharing:** who will be told what, and how quickly
- **Lost wages:** if the surrogate needs to stop working earlier than planned, her documented lost income can be reimbursed according to the agreement
- **Childcare and housekeeping:** help at home if she is on restricted activity or bed rest
- **Travel and accommodation:** for extra appointments, specialist visits, or a hospital far from home
- **Medications and supplies:** such as glucose testing supplies or blood pressure monitors that are not otherwise covered
- **Insurance:** many agreements require life insurance for the surrogate and address what happens with other coverage. See [life and health insurance for surrogates](/knowledge-centre/life-and-health-insurance-for-surrogates).
- **Serious outcomes:** what happens in rare cases such as loss of the pregnancy or effects on future fertility

Your surrogate has her own independent lawyer, and you have yours. They will walk you each through these sections.

## Reimbursement, not payment

Under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*, surrogates in Canada cannot be paid for carrying a pregnancy. A complication does not change that. What a complication can change is the amount of **receipted, pregnancy-related expenses** the intended parents reimburse, usually through an escrow or trust account.

A few honest tips:

- **Intended parents,** build a cushion into your budget for the unexpected. Our [surrogacy cost guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-cost-canada-2026) explains how a typical journey adds up.
- **Surrogates,** keep every receipt and every doctor's note. Documentation makes reimbursement simple.
- **Everyone,** talk early about what happens if bed rest or early leave comes up. It is so much easier to plan before it happens.

See the [surrogacy reimbursements guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-reimbursements-canada-guide) for more on receipts and escrow.

## Taking care of each other emotionally

Complications are scary for everyone. Surrogates sometimes feel guilty, as if they let the intended parents down. They did not. Intended parents sometimes feel helpless, because they cannot be the one in the hospital bed.

What helps:

- Check in on the surrogate as a person, not only on the baby.
- Lean on your agency to help with logistics, so the surrogate can rest.
- Use counselling if it is available. See [what emotional support surrogates get](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-emotional-support-do-surrogates-get).
