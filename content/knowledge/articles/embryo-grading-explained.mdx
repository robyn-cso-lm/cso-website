The embryology report arrives, and it looks like a secret code. "Day 5: 4AB. Day 6: 3BB, 5BC." You start googling at midnight and end up more anxious than when you started.

I understand. I have had intended parents call me in tears over a single letter. So let me explain what these grades mean, and just as importantly, what they do not mean.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Grading systems vary between labs, and your embryologist or doctor is the right person to interpret your own embryos.*

## Why embryos are graded

After eggs are fertilized, embryos grow in the lab for several days. Most clinics now grow them to the blastocyst stage, around day 5 to 7. Before freezing or transferring, embryologists look at each blastocyst under a microscope and give it a grade based on how it looks.

Grading helps the clinic decide which embryos to freeze and which one to transfer first. It is a useful tool. It is not a crystal ball.

## The parts of a blastocyst

A blastocyst has three main parts:

- **The cavity**, a fluid-filled space in the middle that grows as the embryo develops.
- **The inner cell mass (ICM)**, a tight cluster of cells that will become the baby.
- **The trophectoderm (TE)**, the ring of cells around the outside that will become the placenta.

Most Canadian clinics use a grading system based on these three parts. It is often written as a number followed by two letters, like 4AA.

## The number: expansion

The number describes how expanded and developed the blastocyst is. A common scale runs from 1 to 6:

| Number | What it generally means |
|---|---|
| 1 | Early blastocyst, small cavity |
| 2 | Cavity is about half the embryo |
| 3 | Full blastocyst, cavity fills the embryo |
| 4 | Expanded, the embryo is getting bigger and its shell is thinning |
| 5 | Hatching, starting to break out of its shell |
| 6 | Fully hatched |

Higher numbers usually mean the embryo is further along. A 3 on day 5 is not "worse" in a moral sense. It may simply be developing on its own schedule.

## The first letter: inner cell mass

The first letter grades the inner cell mass, the cells that become the baby.

- **A**: many cells, tightly packed
- **B**: several cells, more loosely grouped
- **C**: very few cells

## The second letter: trophectoderm

The second letter grades the outer layer, which becomes the placenta.

- **A**: many cells forming a smooth, even layer
- **B**: fewer cells, a looser layer
- **C**: very few cells

So a **4AA** is an expanded blastocyst with an excellent inner cell mass and an excellent outer layer. A **3BB** is a full blastocyst with good, but not top, ratings for both. A **5BC** is a hatching blastocyst with a good inner cell mass and a weaker outer layer.

## Day 5, day 6, day 7

Some embryos reach the blastocyst stage on day 5. Others take until day 6, and a few until day 7. Many clinics freeze good embryos from all of those days. In general terms, embryos that reach blastocyst earlier tend to do a bit better on average, but day 6 embryos result in many healthy pregnancies. Ask your clinic how they view day 7 embryos, as practices vary.

## What grades do not tell you

This is the part I wish every intended parent read first.

- **Grades are a visual snapshot.** An embryologist is looking at shape and cell numbers at one moment in time. That is valuable, but it is not a genetic test.
- **Grades do not tell you about chromosomes.** A beautiful 4AA embryo can have a chromosome problem, and a modest 3BC can be chromosomally normal. Only genetic testing, such as PGT-A, gives that information, and it has its own limits. See [PGT-A embryo testing explained](/knowledge-centre/pgt-a-embryo-testing-explained).
- **Grades do not predict the health, looks, or personality of a child.** Not even a little.
- **Grading is partly subjective.** Different embryologists, and different labs, can grade the same embryo slightly differently. Some labs use their own scales or add numbers instead of letters.
- **Lower grades can and do become babies.** I have seen many families whose children came from embryos that were not the "best" on paper. Higher grades tend to have better odds on average, but averages are not destinies.

## How clinics use grades

When deciding which embryo to transfer, clinics generally look at a combination of things:

- The grade
- The day it reached blastocyst
- Genetic testing results, if done
- Your history and age at the time the eggs were retrieved

Embryos with higher grades are usually transferred first. If you have tested embryos, the genetic result often matters more than small differences in grade.

## Embryo grading and surrogacy

In surrogacy, families naturally want to give each transfer the best possible chance, because each one involves their surrogate's body and time. That is a kind and responsible instinct. Ask your clinic which embryo they would recommend first and why, and share that reasoning with your surrogate if you are both comfortable. Our FAQ on [what happens if an embryo transfer fails](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-if-an-embryo-transfer-fails) covers the hard days too.

## Questions to ask your embryologist

- Which grading system does your lab use?
- How do you decide which embryo to transfer first?
- Do you freeze day 6 and day 7 embryos?
- Would you recommend genetic testing in my case?
- How do my embryos compare to what you would expect for my age?

## A gentle reminder

You are allowed to feel disappointed when a report is not what you hoped for. You are also allowed to hope. An embryo grade is one piece of information, not the end of your story. Every one of those little embryos is someone's maybe, and maybes are worth holding onto.
