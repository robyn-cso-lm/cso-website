If you are thinking about surrogacy or donation, you have probably run into strong opinions. A friend who thinks it is beautiful. A relative who thinks it is wrong. An article that calls it exploitation, and another that calls it a miracle.

You are allowed to have questions. In fact, I think the families who wrestle with the ethics before they start usually have the healthiest journeys.

I grew up in this field. My mother started Canadian Surrogacy Options in 1992, I was an egg donor five times, and I have spent my life watching these journeys up close. I do not think the hard questions should be brushed aside. So let me lay them out as fairly as I can.

*This article is general information, not legal advice.*

## What "third-party reproduction" means

Third-party reproduction means someone other than the intended parents helps create a baby. That includes:

- A **gestational surrogate** who carries the pregnancy
- An **egg donor** or **sperm donor** who provides gametes
- **Embryo donation**, where another family donates embryos they do not need

Each one raises slightly different questions, but the big themes are the same.

## Question 1: Is consent truly free and informed?

**The concern:** A surrogate or donor might feel pressured, by money, by family, or by an agency, or might not fully understand the medical and emotional risks.

**Why it matters:** Pregnancy and egg retrieval are real medical events with real risks. Agreeing to them should be a clear, informed choice.

**How Canada's model responds:**

- Under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*, it is illegal to counsel or induce anyone under 21 to be a surrogate, or to accept her services.
- Surrogates and donors go through medical and psychological screening before they are accepted.
- Surrogates have their own independent lawyer, separate from the intended parents' lawyer. Egg donors receive independent legal advice before signing their agreement.
- A surrogate keeps the right to make decisions about her own body and her own medical care during the pregnancy.

Good practice goes further: giving people time, making it easy to say no, and making sure no one feels they owe anything for being screened.

## Question 2: Should anyone be paid?

**The concern:** Critics of paid surrogacy and donation worry it turns women's bodies and children into products, and that it may pull in people who need the money more than they want to help. Others argue that refusing payment is unfair, because surrogates and donors do real, difficult work, and everyone else in the process is paid.

**Honest truth:** Thoughtful people disagree here. Different countries have landed in very different places. Our overview of [surrogacy laws around the world](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-laws-around-the-world) shows just how wide that range is.

**How Canada's model responds:** Canada chose the altruistic model. The AHRA makes it a crime to pay a surrogate, to pay a donor for eggs or sperm, or to buy or sell embryos. Surrogates and donors can be reimbursed for receipted expenses connected to their journey, so they are not out of pocket, but they do not profit. See [can a surrogate be paid in Canada?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-a-surrogate-be-paid-in-canada) and [can egg donors be paid in Canada?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-egg-donors-be-paid-in-canada)

The trade-off is real: fewer surrogates and donors, longer waits. But many Canadians feel it keeps the focus on helping rather than selling.

## Question 3: Donor anonymity

**The concern:** In the past, many donors were promised permanent anonymity. Today, many donor-conceived adults say knowing where they come from matters deeply to their identity and health. And with consumer DNA tests, true anonymity is close to impossible anyway.

**The tension:** Donors' privacy on one side. Children's right to know their origins on the other.

**Where things stand in Canada:** Canada does not have a national donor registry, and practices vary between clinics, banks and agencies. Many families now choose **known** or **open-identity** donors, where the donor agrees to be contactable later. At Little Miracles, we talk openly with intended parents and donors about what level of future contact everyone is comfortable with, and we encourage everyone to assume that DNA testing may make contact possible one day.

Read more in [donor-conceived people, their rights and DNA tests](/knowledge-centre/donor-conceived-people-rights-and-dna-tests) and [should we use a known or anonymous egg donor?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/known-donor-or-anonymous-donor)

## Question 4: What about the child?

**The concern:** The child cannot consent to how they were made. So the system has to protect their interests: a secure legal family from birth, honesty about their origins, and access to important medical history.

**How Canada's model responds:**

- Provincial law sets out how intended parents become the legal parents, so the child has a clear family. See [parentage and birth registration after surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/parentage-and-birth-registration-after-surrogacy).
- The AHRA bans choosing a baby's sex for non-medical reasons.
- Screening of donors and surrogates protects the child's health.

The piece that law cannot do is honesty at home. Counsellors and donor-conceived adults are clear that children do best when they are told their story early. See [telling your child about surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/telling-your-child-about-surrogacy).

## Question 5: Fairness and access

There are also wider questions. Treatment is expensive, so who gets access? Should public health plans help? Should single people and LGBTQ+ families have equal access? (In Canada, they do.) Is it fair that many international families travel to Canada because their own countries restrict surrogacy?

There are no neat answers here, but they are worth thinking about.

## Where I land

After a lifetime in this work, here is what I believe: third-party reproduction can be deeply ethical when it is built on informed consent, honesty, fair treatment of surrogates and donors, and putting the child's future first. It can go wrong when any of those are missing.

Canada's model is not perfect. Matching takes time, and the rules around expenses can feel confusing. But it protects people in ways that matter, and the families, surrogates and donors I have known have overwhelmingly come away feeling respected.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Am I comfortable with how my surrogate or donor will be treated?
- How do I feel about the level of contact with a donor or surrogate over time?
- How will I tell my child their story?
- Does my agency put consent and wellbeing ahead of speed?
