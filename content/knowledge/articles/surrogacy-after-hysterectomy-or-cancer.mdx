If you are reading this, you may have been through something enormous. A cancer diagnosis. A hysterectomy. A medical team telling you that carrying a pregnancy is not possible or not safe for you. Maybe you are still in treatment. Maybe you are years past it and only now ready to think about building your family.

Whatever brought you here, I want to start by saying this: I am so sorry for what you have been through. And your story is not over.

I grew up in this field. My mother, Joanne Wright, founded Canadian Surrogacy Options in 1992, and I have been around surrogacy since I was 4 years old. Some of the most moving journeys I have ever been part of started exactly where you are right now.

*This article is general information, not medical or legal advice. Your oncologist, fertility clinic and lawyer are the right people for questions about your own situation.*

## First, let yourself grieve

Before we talk about options, I want to make space for this. Losing the ability to carry a pregnancy is a real loss, even when you know it was the right medical decision, and even when you are grateful to be healthy.

You might feel sad, angry, numb or all three. You might feel guilty for grieving when you are lucky to be alive. Please let yourself feel it. To grieve is to know love, and that includes love for the family you are still hoping to build.

Many intended parents find it helps to talk with a counsellor who understands fertility and loss before or during the surrogacy process. You do not have to be "over it" to start. You just have to be ready to take the next step.

## How surrogacy works when you cannot carry

In Canada, surrogacy is gestational. That means the surrogate carries an embryo created through IVF, and she is not genetically related to the baby. You can learn the basics in [what is surrogacy in Canada?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-is-surrogacy-in-canada) and [gestational vs traditional surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/faqs/gestational-vs-traditional-surrogacy).

The embryo can be created with:

- **Your own eggs**, if you have eggs or embryos stored, or if you still have ovaries and your care team says egg retrieval is possible and safe for you
- **Donor eggs**, if using your own eggs is not possible or not recommended
- **Your partner's sperm or donor sperm**, depending on your family

## Using your own eggs

A hysterectomy removes the uterus, but it does not always remove the ovaries. Some people still have working ovaries after a hysterectomy, and some had eggs or embryos frozen before cancer treatment.

If that is you, your fertility clinic can talk with you about whether those eggs or embryos can be used, and whether a new egg retrieval might be possible. If you have a history of cancer, your fertility doctor will want to work closely with your oncology team, because some treatments and some cancers affect what is safe for you.

Please do not rule anything in or out until you have had that conversation. I have seen families surprised in both directions.

Our FAQ [can we use our own eggs and sperm?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-we-use-our-own-eggs-and-sperm) has more on this.

## Using donor eggs

If your own eggs are not an option, egg donation is a beautiful path to parenthood. Your child will still be your child in every way that matters. You will be the one who chose to build this family, who dreamed about them, who held their hand.

It is completely normal to grieve the genetic connection before you feel ready to embrace donor eggs. Take the time you need.

When you are ready, our sister company Little Miracles has been matching families with egg donors since 2007. You can learn more in [everything you need to know about egg donation in Canada](/knowledge-centre/egg-donation-in-canada), [how to choose an egg donor](/knowledge-centre/how-to-choose-an-egg-donor) and [can you combine egg donation and surrogacy?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-you-combine-egg-donation-and-surrogacy)

## Timing after cancer treatment

If you have had cancer, your medical team may want you to wait a certain amount of time after treatment before starting IVF, or may want to review your health before you begin. This depends entirely on your diagnosis and treatment, and only your doctors can tell you what is right for you.

The good news is that the surrogate is the one carrying the pregnancy, which can open doors that might otherwise be closed. You can start some of the non-medical steps, like learning about agencies and the matching process, while you wait for medical clearance.

## Starting the journey

Here is what the path usually looks like:

1. **Talk to your fertility clinic** about your eggs, embryos or the need for donor eggs.
2. **Learn about surrogacy and talk to an agency** so you understand costs, timelines and how matching works.
3. **Create embryos** if you do not already have them.
4. **Match with a surrogate** who is the right fit for your family.
5. **Legal agreements**, with separate independent lawyers for you and your surrogate.
6. **Embryo transfer, pregnancy and birth.**

You can see realistic timing in our [surrogacy timeline guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-timeline-canada), and learn how matching works in [how does surrogate matching work?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/how-does-surrogate-matching-work)

## What about cost?

Surrogacy in Canada involves agency fees, legal fees, IVF costs and the surrogate's reimbursed expenses. Surrogates in Canada cannot be paid under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*. They are reimbursed for receipted pregnancy-related expenses.

At CSO, our programs start from $1,500 for an Independent Journey and go up to Priority Match from $29,500 (CAD, plus HST). Our agency fee is paid in three equal stages, never all upfront. You can see the full picture in our [surrogacy cost guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-cost-canada-2026).

Depending on where you live, some provinces fund part of IVF. See [which provinces fund IVF in Canada](/knowledge-centre/faqs/which-provinces-fund-ivf-in-canada).
