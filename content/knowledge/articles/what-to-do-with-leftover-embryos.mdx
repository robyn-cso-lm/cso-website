The storage invoice arrives again. You pay it, file it, and push the question to next year. Again.

If that sounds familiar, you are not alone. So many families finish building their family through IVF or surrogacy and find themselves with frozen embryos and no idea what to do with them. Some feel guilty about how long they have waited to decide. Some disagree with their partner. Some just cannot bring themselves to look at it yet.

I want to say this first: there is no wrong feeling here. Those embryos represent hope, money, needles, waiting, and in many cases a whole journey with a surrogate or donor. Of course this is hard. Let me lay out your options plainly so you can make a decision that feels right for your family.

*This article is general information, not legal or medical advice. Your clinic and your lawyer will confirm what applies to you.*

## Your four main options

In Canada, families with leftover embryos generally have four options:

1. Keep them frozen (in storage)
2. Donate them to another person or family
3. Donate them to research
4. Have them discarded (sometimes called "allowing them to thaw")

Some families combine options, for example keeping one or two for a while and donating the rest.

## Option 1: Keep them frozen

Many people keep embryos in storage while they decide, or because they are not sure their family is complete.

What to know:

- **Storage fees** are usually charged annually by your clinic or a storage facility. Fees vary by clinic, so check your own contract.
- **Frozen embryos can be stored for a long time.** Your clinic can talk to you about its own experience with long-stored embryos.
- **Your storage agreement matters.** Many clinics have rules about what happens if fees go unpaid or if they cannot reach you. Read it, and keep your contact information up to date.
- **Moving or changing clinics?** Embryos can often be shipped. See [shipping embryos and gametes in Canada](/knowledge-centre/shipping-embryos-and-gametes-canada).

Keeping embryos frozen is a valid choice. Just make it a choice, not an accident. Put a date in your calendar to revisit the decision each year.

## Option 2: Donate to another family

Some families find real comfort in the idea that their embryos could become someone else's child. Embryo donation can be done through a clinic program, directly to someone you know, or through a matching service.

Things to think through:

- Any child born would be a full genetic sibling to your children.
- You will need to decide how much openness you want: none, information sharing, or ongoing contact.
- You will usually need counselling, medical history updates, possibly blood tests, a legal agreement and independent legal advice.
- Under the Assisted Human Reproduction Act, embryos cannot be sold. You can be reimbursed only for receipted expenses directly related to the donation.

I wrote a full guide to [embryo donation in Canada](/knowledge-centre/embryo-donation-in-canada) that covers both sides of this.

## Option 3: Donate to research

Embryos can be donated for research, for example research that helps improve IVF or studies that use stem cells. In Canada, research using human embryos is permitted only with proper consent and under strict ethics oversight, and you cannot be paid for donating embryos.

What to know:

- Not every clinic participates in research programs. Ask whether yours does, or whether one is available.
- You will usually sign specific research consent forms explaining what the embryos may be used for.
- Embryos donated to research are not used to create a pregnancy.

For some families, research donation feels like a meaningful way to help other people who are struggling with infertility.

## Option 4: Discard

Some families choose to have their embryos discarded. The clinic thaws the embryos and they are no longer viable.

What to know:

- You will sign a consent form, and if the embryos belong to a couple, both people usually need to sign.
- Some clinics allow families to be present, or to hold a small private moment. Ask if that matters to you.
- Some families create their own ritual: a candle, a letter, planting something. There is no right or wrong way to mark it.

Choosing to discard is not a failure, and it does not mean the embryos did not matter. It is simply a decision that the journey is complete.

## Consent forms: what you signed before

Before IVF, you almost certainly signed consent forms that included what should happen to embryos in certain situations, like separation, divorce, or the death of one partner. Please dig those out and read them again.

A few honest points:

- **Your consent can usually be changed or withdrawn** before the embryos are used, but there are rules about how. Ask your clinic.
- **Both partners typically have to agree.** If you are separated and cannot agree, the embryos usually stay in storage until there is agreement or a legal resolution. Speak with a family lawyer.
- **If donor eggs or donor sperm were used**, the original donor's consent or agreement may limit what you can do, especially with onward donation. If you used a Little Miracles egg donor, your ovum donation agreement will say what was agreed. See [the ovum donation agreement and independent legal advice](/knowledge-centre/ovum-donation-agreement-canada).
- **If you worked with a surrogate,** your surrogacy agreement may also say something about remaining embryos.

## The emotional side

This is where families tend to get stuck, so let me be gentle and honest.

- **It is okay to grieve.** Even when your family is complete, closing the door on future children is a real loss. Something I always come back to: to grieve is to know love.
- **Partners often feel differently.** One may be ready to move on while the other is not. That is normal. Talk it through, and consider a session with a fertility counsellor.
- **Your values matter more than anyone's opinion.** Your faith, your culture and your own sense of what those embryos mean should guide this decision.
- **You do not have to decide today.** But you do not need to wait for it to feel easy either. It may never feel easy. It can still feel right.

If you are carrying grief from your fertility journey more broadly, our article on [infertility grief and mental health](/knowledge-centre/infertility-grief-and-mental-health) may help.

## Questions to ask yourselves

- Do we feel our family is complete? Are we sure, or are we "probably" sure?
- How would we feel about a full genetic sibling of our children growing up in another family?
- Would we want any contact or information about a child born from our embryos?
- What do our beliefs say about research or discarding?
- How long are we willing to keep paying for storage while we decide?
