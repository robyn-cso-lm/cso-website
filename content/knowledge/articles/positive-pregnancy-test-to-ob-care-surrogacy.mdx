The two week wait is over, the call comes in, and the news is good. Whether you are the surrogate or the intended parents, this is one of those moments you will remember forever.

It is also the start of a new stage with its own steps, its own waiting and its own nerves. Let me walk you through what usually happens between that first positive result and the day care moves from the fertility clinic to an OB or midwife.

I grew up in this field, and I have been part of surrogacy journeys since I was 4 years old. I have seen so many families hold their breath through these early weeks. Knowing what is coming helps.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Every clinic has its own protocol, and your fertility clinic and care provider are the right people for questions about your own pregnancy.*

## The first blood test (the "beta")

After an embryo transfer, the fertility clinic usually books a blood test to measure pregnancy hormone levels. People in the fertility world call this "the beta."

Some surrogates take home pregnancy tests before the beta. That is a personal choice, and it is worth talking with the intended parents about it ahead of time. Some families want to know right away. Others would rather wait for the clinic's result together. Our article on tips for the two week wait can help everyone get through those days.

Your clinic may ask for a second blood test a few days later to see how the levels are changing. What the clinic looks for, and what the numbers mean, is something your clinic will explain. I will not quote numbers here, because every pregnancy is different and the clinic is the right one to read them.

## Who gets the news, and how

This sounds small, but it matters. Decide before the beta who the clinic will call and how the news gets shared. Often the surrogate hears first and calls the intended parents, or everyone hops on a call together.

Talk about it in advance so nobody finds out in a way that stings.

## Medications continue for a while

Most surrogates continue some of their transfer medications into early pregnancy, as directed by the clinic. This often includes hormone support for a number of weeks.

Surrogates: do not stop or change any medications unless the clinic tells you to, even when you feel great. If you have side effects or questions, call your clinic. You can read more in our surrogacy medications and IVF protocol guide.

## The first ultrasound

A few weeks after the positive beta, the clinic usually books an early ultrasound. This is often the first chance to see the pregnancy, confirm where it is and, a little later, see a heartbeat.

This appointment is a big deal for intended parents. Talk ahead of time about whether they will attend in person, join by video call if the clinic allows it, or wait for photos and an update. Clinic rules about guests can vary, so check first.

Sometimes there is more than one ultrasound in this early stage. Your clinic will let you know their schedule.

## Hard news can happen too

I promised radical honesty, so here it is: not every positive test leads to an ongoing pregnancy. Early loss can happen in any pregnancy, including surrogacy pregnancies, and it is no one's fault.

If it happens, everyone involved will need care and time. The surrogate is going through a physical loss. The intended parents are grieving a hope they held close. To grieve is to know love.

Our articles on [what happens if an embryo transfer fails](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-if-an-embryo-transfer-fails) and [when surrogacy does not go as planned](/knowledge-centre/when-surrogacy-does-not-go-as-planned) talk about next steps, gently.

## Graduating from the fertility clinic

Once the pregnancy is well established, the fertility clinic "graduates" the surrogate to regular prenatal care. Many people find this moment surprisingly emotional. It means the pregnancy is moving forward, and it also means leaving a team who has been with you through the hardest part.

From here, prenatal care is usually with an OB, a midwife or a family doctor who provides obstetric care, depending on what is available where the surrogate lives and what she prefers.

A few tips for this handoff:

- **Line up a care provider early.** In some communities, OB and midwife spots fill quickly. It is worth getting a referral in place before graduation day.
- **Make sure records get transferred.** The fertility clinic will usually send information to the new provider. It does not hurt to double check.
- **Tell the new provider it is a surrogacy pregnancy.** It helps them understand who the intended parents are and how information may be shared with them, based on the surrogate's consent.

## How involved can intended parents be?

During these early weeks, many intended parents wonder how much they can be part of things. The answer depends on the relationship you have built together and what the surrogate is comfortable with.

Our FAQ on [how involved intended parents can be in the pregnancy](/knowledge-centre/faqs/how-involved-can-intended-parents-be-in-the-pregnancy) covers this, and our article on keeping intended parents involved has lots of ideas.

## When to share the news

Many families wait until later in the first trimester to announce. Others share right away with close family. There is no rule. Just make sure the surrogate and the intended parents agree, especially about social media, since this is both of their stories.

## A quick checklist for these early weeks

- Agree on how the beta result will be shared
- Keep taking medications exactly as prescribed
- Plan who will attend the early ultrasound
- Line up an OB, midwife or family doctor for after graduation
- Keep receipts for travel, parking and other pregnancy-related costs
- Agree on when and how to share the news
