Sooner or later, almost every family I talk to asks some version of this: "Should we do surrogacy in Canada or in the States?"

It is a great question, and I am in an unusual position to answer it, because I work on both sides of the border. Canadian Surrogacy Options has helped Canadian and international families with altruistic surrogacy since my mother founded it in 1992. Camica is our US-facing program for families who want compensated surrogacy. So I do not have a reason to push you one way. I just want you to pick the path that fits your family.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Laws differ by province and by US state, and your lawyer will confirm what applies to you.*

## The big difference: altruistic vs compensated

**In Canada, surrogacy is altruistic.** The federal *Assisted Human Reproduction Act* makes it a crime to pay a surrogate for carrying a baby. Surrogates are reimbursed for receipted, pregnancy-related expenses, and that is it. Women become surrogates here because they want to help a family.

**In many US states, surrogacy is compensated.** The surrogate receives a fee for carrying the pregnancy, on top of her expenses. Laws are set state by state. Some states have detailed surrogacy laws and are known as very surrogacy friendly. Others restrict it or have unclear rules. A few states have only allowed compensated surrogacy fairly recently. New York, for example, changed its law in 2021.

Neither model is "better." They are built on different values, and they lead to different experiences.

## Side by side

| | Canada | United States |
|---|---|---|
| Surrogate paid? | No. Receipted expense reimbursement only | Yes, in states that allow it, plus expenses |
| Typical total budget | About $80K to $120K CAD | Usually much higher. Surrogate compensation alone is often $55,000 to $70,000+ USD |
| Matching speed | Can be slow. Fewer surrogates than families | Often faster, depending on the agency and your preferences |
| Surrogate's medical care | Usually covered by her provincial health plan | Needs private insurance arranged for the surrogate |
| Legal parentage | Set by provincial law | Set by state law. Many states allow orders before or soon after birth |
| Baby's citizenship | Born in Canada, so a Canadian citizen | Born in the US, so a US citizen. Canadian citizenship is a separate application |
| Travel | Little or none for Canadian families | Travel for transfer, birth and paperwork |

## Cost

In Canada, a typical full surrogacy journey costs about $80K to $120K CAD, including IVF, legal fees, agency fees and the surrogate's reimbursed expenses. Our [surrogacy cost breakdown](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-cost-canada-2026) walks through every line. CSO's agency programs start from $1,500 for our Independent Journey up to from $29,500 for Priority Match (CAD + HST), and the agency fee is always paid in three equal stages, never all upfront.

In the US, the surrogate's compensation, private insurance for the pregnancy, hospital costs for the baby, escrow management, legal work in the surrogate's state and travel all add up. Total costs are usually significantly higher than in Canada, and they vary a lot by state and agency. For reference, Camica's agency fees are $28,000 (Signature), $38,000 (Priority) and $48,000 (Premier) USD, with surrogate compensation of about $55,000 to $70,000+ USD and medical, legal and escrow costs separate. See [how much does Camica cost?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/how-much-does-camica-cost) for the full picture.

## Matching speed

This is where the honest truth matters most.

In Canada, there are more families looking for a surrogate than there are women ready to carry. Because surrogates are not paid, the pool is smaller. That means waits for a match can be long, sometimes many months, sometimes longer. A strong profile, flexibility on location, and an agency with an active surrogate community all help. See [how long does it take to be matched with a surrogate?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/how-long-does-it-take-to-be-matched-with-a-surrogate)

In the US, compensation brings more women forward, so matches often happen faster, though it still depends on the agency and on what you are looking for in a surrogate.

## Legal certainty

**In Canada,** parentage is decided province by province. In most provinces, intended parents become the legal parents through a court declaration or an administrative process after the birth. The details vary, so read [the legal framework for surrogacy in Canada](/knowledge-centre/legal-framework-canada) and our guide to [parentage and birth registration after surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/parentage-and-birth-registration-after-surrogacy). Both the surrogate and the intended parents have independent lawyers.

**In the US,** surrogacy-friendly states often have well established processes, and many allow a parentage order before the birth. That can give families real peace of mind. But "the US" is not one system. The state where the surrogate lives and gives birth matters a great deal, and you need a lawyer licensed there.

**For Canadian families doing US surrogacy,** there is an extra layer: your baby will be a US citizen by birth, and you will need to apply for Canadian citizenship and a Canadian passport before bringing them home to live. Talk to a Canadian immigration lawyer early about how citizenship by descent applies to your family.

## What the experience feels like

Both models can be beautiful. Some differences I see:

- **Canadian journeys** often feel very relationship driven. Surrogates choose families they connect with, and many stay close for years.
- **US journeys** are often more structured and faster, with clear contracts around compensation. Many US surrogates also form wonderful bonds with their families.
- **Healthcare is different.** In Canada, the surrogate's prenatal and delivery care is usually through the public system. In the US, insurance is a big planning item.

## Which is right for you?

Canada may fit best if:

- You live in Canada and want to stay close to your surrogate and the pregnancy
- Budget is a major factor
- You value the altruistic model and the relationships it tends to create
- You can be patient with matching

The US may fit best if:

- Time matters most, for example because of age or embryo storage
- You have a bigger budget
- You want a compensated model with very defined contracts
- You are comfortable travelling and working with US lawyers

Some families start in Canada and open a US path at the same time. Others do the reverse. The right choice is the one you can live with, financially and emotionally.
