If you have looked at the cost of egg donation and felt your stomach drop a little, you are not alone. Many families come to me wanting donor eggs and wondering how on earth they will make the numbers work, especially if they are also paying for IVF, or planning a surrogacy journey on top of it.

One option I always want families to know about is a shared-cost cycle. It is not right for everyone, but for some families it is the thing that makes this possible.

I grew up in this field, I donated my own eggs five times, and I have run Little Miracles since 2007. Here is the honest version.

*This article is general information, not legal or medical advice. Laws and clinic policies vary, so please speak with your fertility clinic and a fertility lawyer about your own situation.*

## What "shared-cost" means

In a shared-cost cycle, **two recipient families share one donor's cycle.** The donor goes through one stimulation and one egg retrieval. The eggs retrieved are divided between the two families, and some of the costs are split between them instead of one family carrying everything.

That is the general idea. The specific details, like which costs are shared, how eggs are divided and what happens in different scenarios, depend on the program and the clinic. At Little Miracles, we walk every family through exactly how our shared-cost option works before anyone commits, so there are no surprises.

## Why families choose it

The biggest reason is cost. When certain costs of a donor cycle are shared, each family's portion can be lower than doing a full cycle on their own.

For context, a full Little Miracles egg donation (on the matching and donor side, not counting your clinic) is estimated at about $16,450 to $20,400 CAD, and higher for a non-local donor. That includes an agency fee from $2,750 to $3,500 plus HST, donor expenses of about $5,500 to $8,000, ovum agreement legal fees of about $1,350 to $1,550, donor ILA of $550, and psychological evaluations at about $400 each. You can see the full breakdown in our [egg donation costs guide](/knowledge-centre/egg-donation-costs-in-canada).

Every situation is different, so I will not promise a specific saving. What I can do is go through the numbers with you for your own case.

## The honest trade-offs

I would not be doing my job if I only told you the good part. Here is what to think about.

**You will receive fewer eggs than in a full cycle.** Because the eggs from one retrieval are being divided, each family receives a share rather than the whole. That can mean fewer embryos and possibly fewer chances from that one cycle. Talk with your clinic about what that might mean for you, especially if you hope to have more than one child from the same donor.

**You are linked to another family's choices.** You both need to be comfortable with the same donor, and the timing has to work for everyone, including the donor and the clinic.

**There may be genetic siblings in another family.** If both families have children from the same donor, those children are genetic half-siblings. Some families find that meaningful. Others feel unsure. There is no wrong answer, but it is something to think about now rather than later.

**Things do not always go to plan.** A donor's cycle can have fewer eggs than hoped, or a cycle may need to be cancelled for medical reasons. Ask in advance how those situations are handled.

## Is it legal in Canada?

Shared donor cycles can be arranged in Canada, and the same core law applies as in any donation: under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*, a donor **cannot be paid** for her eggs. She can only be reimbursed for receipted, donation-related expenses. That does not change because two families are involved.

The legal paperwork does need extra care. Each family will need its own agreement terms covering their share, and the donor still has her own independent lawyer. Your agreement should be clear about how eggs are allocated, what happens to any unused eggs or embryos, and expectations about contact and disclosure. You can read more about how that works in [The ovum donation agreement and independent legal advice, explained](/knowledge-centre/ovum-donation-agreement-canada).

Provincial rules about parentage and paperwork differ, so please speak with a fertility lawyer in your province.

## Questions to ask before you say yes

Bring these to your consult with us and to your clinic:

1. Which costs are shared, and which ones am I responsible for on my own?
2. How will the eggs be divided between the families?
3. What happens if fewer eggs are retrieved than expected, or the cycle is cancelled?
4. Will the other family and I know anything about each other?
5. Can I go back to the same donor in the future if I want a sibling?
6. How do the clinic's own fees work in a shared cycle?
7. What do the legal agreements cover, and who drafts them?

If the answers make you feel calm and informed, that is a good sign. If they make you feel rushed or confused, pause and ask again. You deserve clarity.

## Who shared-cost tends to suit

In my experience, shared-cost cycles tend to work well for families who:

- Want to reduce their upfront costs
- Are comfortable with the idea of genetic half-siblings in another family
- Are flexible on timing
- Have talked through the possibility of fewer embryos and made peace with it

It may not be the best fit if you are hoping to build a larger family from one cycle, or if the idea of another family having children from your donor does not sit well with you. That is okay, too. A full cycle may be the better path.
