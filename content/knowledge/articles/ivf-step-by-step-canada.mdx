If you have just been told IVF is your next step, or you have realized that surrogacy or donor eggs will involve IVF, you are probably staring at a calendar full of words you have never used before. Stimulation. Trigger. Retrieval. Blastocyst. Beta.

Take a breath. The basic shape of an IVF cycle is the same almost everywhere, and once you know it, every appointment makes more sense.

I grew up in this field, and I have walked hundreds of families through IVF as part of their surrogacy or egg donation journeys. I was also an egg donor five times, so I know the needles and the waiting room chairs from the inside. Here is the honest, plain-language version.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Every clinic has its own protocols, and your fertility doctor will tell you what applies to you.*

## Step 1: The first consult and testing

Your journey starts with a consultation at a fertility clinic. The doctor reviews your history and orders tests. Typical ones include:

- **Bloodwork** for hormones such as AMH (a marker of egg supply), plus infectious disease screening.
- **An ultrasound** to look at the uterus and count the small follicles in each ovary.
- **A semen analysis** if sperm from a partner will be used.
- **Other tests** depending on your history, such as genetic carrier screening.

From these results, the doctor builds your treatment plan, including which medications you will take and at what doses. In Canada, you may also wait some time for a first appointment, especially if you are trying to access a provincially funded cycle. Our FAQ on [which provinces help pay for IVF](/knowledge-centre/faqs/which-provinces-fund-ivf-in-canada) explains the funding picture.

## Step 2: Getting ready for the cycle

Many protocols start with a "prep" phase. Some clinics use birth control pills or other medications in the weeks before stimulation to help time the cycle and keep follicles growing evenly. Others start right at the beginning of a natural period. Your clinic will give you a calendar, and it is worth keeping it somewhere you will see it every day.

## Step 3: Ovarian stimulation

This is the part most people picture when they hear "IVF." For roughly 8 to 14 days, the person providing the eggs takes daily hormone injections. These medications encourage several follicles to grow at once, instead of the single egg a body usually releases each month.

Other medications are added partway through to stop the body from ovulating on its own before the eggs can be collected.

Expect some tiredness, bloating and moodiness, and plan a gentle couple of weeks.

## Step 4: Monitoring appointments

During stimulation, you will go to the clinic often, sometimes every day or two near the end. Each visit usually includes an early morning blood test and a vaginal ultrasound to measure the follicles. Your doses may change based on these results.

These appointments are early and frequent, which matters if you work or live far from the clinic. Some clinics allow part of the monitoring to happen at a closer satellite clinic.

## Step 5: The trigger shot

When enough follicles reach the right size, the clinic tells you to take a "trigger" injection at a very specific time, usually late in the evening. This shot does the final ripening of the eggs. Egg retrieval is then booked for about 36 hours later, so timing really matters. Set two alarms.

## Step 6: Egg retrieval

Retrieval is a short procedure, usually done under sedation. The doctor uses an ultrasound-guided needle through the vaginal wall to draw fluid out of each follicle, and the embryology lab finds the eggs in that fluid. You go home the same day and will need someone to drive you.

Cramping and bloating for a few days afterward are common. Call your clinic right away if you have severe pain, rapid weight gain, or trouble breathing, as these can be signs of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome. Our FAQ on [what OHSS is](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-is-ohss) explains more.

## Step 7: Fertilization

On retrieval day, the lab combines the mature eggs with sperm. There are two main methods:

- **Conventional IVF**, where eggs and sperm are placed together in a dish.
- **ICSI**, where an embryologist injects a single sperm into each mature egg. This is common when there are sperm concerns, and many clinics use it routinely.

Not every egg retrieved will be mature, and not every mature egg will fertilize. That drop-off is completely normal and expected.

## Step 8: Embryo development

The fertilized eggs grow in the lab for several days. Most clinics now grow embryos to the blastocyst stage, around day 5 to 7, because it gives a better picture of which embryos are developing well. Again, not every embryo will make it to that stage. The clinic will usually call you with updates, and those calls can be hard. Our article on [embryo grading](/knowledge-centre/embryo-grading-explained) explains what the lab is looking at.

## Step 9: Optional genetic testing

Some families choose to have a few cells taken from each blastocyst for genetic testing before freezing. This is called PGT-A (for chromosome counts) or PGT-M (for a specific inherited condition). It is optional, it adds cost, and it has real limits. Read [PGT-A embryo testing explained](/knowledge-centre/pgt-a-embryo-testing-explained) before you decide.

## Step 10: Freezing or fresh transfer

Embryos can be transferred fresh, a few days after retrieval, or frozen using a fast-freezing method called vitrification and transferred in a later cycle. Many clinics now freeze all embryos and transfer later, especially when PGT testing is done or when the body needs time to recover from stimulation.

## Step 11: Embryo transfer

Transfer is quick and usually does not need sedation. Using an ultrasound for guidance, the doctor passes a thin catheter through the cervix and places the embryo in the uterus. Most Canadian clinics transfer one embryo at a time, because twin pregnancies carry more risk for both the pregnant person and the babies.

For a frozen embryo, the uterus is prepared beforehand, either with medications or by following a natural cycle. See [frozen embryo transfer protocols](/knowledge-centre/frozen-embryo-transfer-protocols) for how that works.

## Step 12: The pregnancy test

About 9 to 14 days after transfer, depending on the clinic, a blood test called a beta hCG checks for pregnancy. If it is positive, you will usually have a repeat test and then an early ultrasound. Our guide to [beta hCG levels](/knowledge-centre/beta-hcg-levels-explained) walks through what those numbers can and cannot tell you.

## How IVF fits with surrogacy

In gestational surrogacy, IVF is split between two people:

- **The person providing the eggs** (an intended parent or an egg donor) goes through steps 1 to 7.
- **The embryos** are created and usually frozen.
- **The surrogate** goes through her own screening and then a uterine prep cycle, and the embryo is transferred into her uterus.

In Canada, embryos for surrogacy are usually created and frozen before transfer. Health Canada has safety rules for using eggs or sperm in someone other than the person they came from, and your clinic will explain what screening and timelines those rules mean for you. Our article on [how many embryo transfers it usually takes](/knowledge-centre/faqs/how-many-embryo-transfers-does-it-take) is helpful here, and so is the [surrogacy timeline guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-timeline-canada).

## How IVF fits with donor eggs

With donor eggs, the donor does the stimulation and retrieval, and the eggs are fertilized with the intended parent's or a donor's sperm. The resulting embryos are then transferred to the intended mother or to a surrogate. Donor eggs may be fresh from a cycle or previously frozen, and each route has trade-offs, which we explain in [fresh vs frozen donor eggs](/knowledge-centre/fresh-vs-frozen-donor-eggs).

If you are not sure which pieces you need, [Do I need an egg donor, a surrogate, or both?](/knowledge-centre/do-i-need-an-egg-donor-a-surrogate-or-both) is a good place to start.

## One honest truth

The numbers shrink at every step, and some families need more than one cycle. That is normal. Lean on your people while you go through it.
