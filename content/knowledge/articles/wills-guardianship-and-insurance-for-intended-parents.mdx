Nobody starts a surrogacy journey wanting to think about their own death. You are thinking about nursery colours, transfer dates and whether the first ultrasound will show a heartbeat.

But here is the honest truth from someone who grew up in this field: one of the most loving things you can do for your future child, and for the woman carrying them, is to get your paperwork in order before the transfer. It takes a few appointments. It protects everyone.

*This article is general information, not legal or financial advice. A lawyer in your province and a licensed insurance or financial advisor will confirm what is right for your family.*

## Why surrogacy makes this more important

In a typical pregnancy, if something happened to the parents, the baby's legal parent (the person who gave birth) would still be there. In surrogacy, that is not the plan. The surrogate has agreed to carry a baby who is not hers to raise.

So the question "who would raise this baby if we could not?" needs a real, written answer. Without one, a court may have to decide, and your surrogate could be left in an incredibly hard position.

That is why most Canadian surrogacy agreements require intended parents to:

- Have a valid will in place.
- Name a guardian for the child.
- Carry some form of life insurance, or otherwise show how the child and the surrogate's expenses would be provided for.

Your lawyer will explain the exact wording in your agreement. You can learn more in [what is a surrogacy agreement?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-is-a-surrogacy-agreement)

## Your will

If you do not have a will, now is the time. If you do, it probably needs updating. Things to talk through with your lawyer:

- **Including a child who is not born yet.** Wills can be written to include children born or conceived after the will is signed, but the wording matters, especially when assisted reproduction is involved. Ask your lawyer to make sure a child born through surrogacy is clearly included.
- **What happens if you both die before parentage is finalized.** Parentage after a surrogacy birth is not always instant. See [parentage and birth registration after surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/parentage-and-birth-registration-after-surrogacy). Your lawyer can help plan for the gap.
- **Executors and trustees.** Who will manage money for your child until they are grown?
- **Your surrogate's expenses.** If the worst happened during the pregnancy, who would keep paying her eligible expenses and medical costs? Many agreements expect the intended parents' estate or insurance to cover this.

## Naming a guardian

A guardian is the person (or couple) who would raise your child if you could not. Some things to think about:

- Have an honest conversation with them first. Do they understand how your child came into the world, and are they comfortable with an ongoing relationship with your surrogate if that is what you would want?
- Name a backup in case your first choice cannot do it.
- Write down your wishes about how you want your child to learn their story. Our article on [telling your child about surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/telling-your-child-about-surrogacy) can help you think about this.
- If you live outside Canada, ask how a guardianship appointment would be recognized in both countries.

Some surrogacy agreements ask for the guardian to be named in the agreement itself, or ask for the guardian to confirm they are willing. Your lawyer will guide you.

## Your embryos

This is the part people forget. Your clinic consent forms will ask what should happen to your frozen embryos if one or both of you die, if you separate, or if you stop paying storage. Under Canadian law, consent for the use of embryos must be in writing, so those forms carry real weight.

- Read every option slowly.
- Make sure your consent forms and your will do not contradict each other.
- If your choices change, ask the clinic how to update the forms.

If you are not sure what you would want, read [what to do with leftover embryos](/knowledge-centre/what-to-do-with-leftover-embryos) before you sign.

## Life insurance for you

Life insurance is about making sure your child, and your surrogacy commitments, are covered if something happens to you.

Questions to ask a licensed advisor:

- How much coverage would raise a child to adulthood, including education?
- Is your existing workplace coverage enough, and does it stay with you if you change jobs?
- Would term or permanent insurance make more sense for your family?
- Who should be the beneficiary, and should money for a child go into a trust?

I am not a licensed insurance advisor, so I will not tell you which product to buy. What I will tell you is that families who sort this out early feel lighter for the rest of the journey.

## Insurance for your surrogate

Separately from your own coverage, intended parents in Canada commonly buy a life insurance policy on the surrogate for the length of the journey, so her family is protected if something tragic happened. Some agreements also address disability coverage. I have written a full article on that: [life and health insurance for surrogates](/knowledge-centre/life-and-health-insurance-for-surrogates).

## Other documents worth updating

- **Powers of attorney** for property and personal care, in case you are unable to make decisions.
- **Beneficiaries** on RRSPs, TFSAs, pensions and existing insurance policies. These often pass outside a will, so update them directly.
- **An RESP** for your baby once they are born, if that fits your plans.
- **A simple contact sheet** for your guardian, executor and surrogate, so the people who need to reach each other can.

## A timeline that works

- **Before you sign your surrogacy agreement:** meet with a wills lawyer, name your guardian, speak to an insurance advisor.
- **Before transfer:** have the will signed, insurance in place (yours and your surrogate's), and clinic consent forms reviewed.
- **After the birth:** update beneficiaries, add your child to your benefits, and review whether anything needs adjusting now that your child is here.

Your surrogacy lawyer may or may not also do wills. If not, they can usually refer you to someone who does.
