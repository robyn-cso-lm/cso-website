Your clinic has asked whether you want to test your embryos, and you have about a week to decide. Maybe you have heard that PGT-A is the smart choice. Maybe a friend told you it is a waste of money. Maybe you just want someone to explain it in plain words.

That is what I am here for. I have sat beside a lot of intended parents making this exact decision, especially families heading into surrogacy, where every transfer involves another person's body, time and heart. There is no single right answer, but there is a well-informed one.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your fertility doctor and, where offered, a genetic counsellor are the right people to help you decide for your own embryos.*

## What PGT actually is

PGT stands for preimplantation genetic testing. It means testing embryos created through IVF before one is transferred. There are a few kinds:

- **PGT-A** (aneuploidy): checks whether an embryo appears to have the expected number of chromosomes. Embryos with missing or extra chromosomes are more likely to not implant, to miscarry, or in some cases to result in a chromosomal condition.
- **PGT-M** (monogenic): looks for one specific inherited condition that runs in a family, such as a gene a parent is known to carry. This requires setting up a custom test in advance, which can take weeks.
- **PGT-SR** (structural rearrangements): used when a parent has a known chromosome rearrangement.

When people say "testing our embryos," they usually mean PGT-A.

## How the testing works

Embryos are grown in the lab to the blastocyst stage, around day 5 to 7. At that point, an embryologist removes a few cells from the outer layer, the part that will later become the placenta. That small sample goes to a genetics lab, and the embryo is frozen while everyone waits for results, which often take one to a few weeks.

The inner part of the embryo, which becomes the baby, is not touched. That is an important point, and it is also the source of one of PGT-A's limits, which I will get to.

## The possible benefits

For some families, PGT-A can be genuinely helpful:

- **Choosing which embryo to transfer first.** If you have several embryos, testing can help prioritize the ones most likely to be chromosomally normal.
- **Possibly fewer failed transfers or miscarriages.** By avoiding embryos that appear abnormal, some families may reach a pregnancy in fewer attempts. This can matter a lot in surrogacy, where each transfer involves your surrogate going through medications and procedures.
- **Information.** If all embryos come back abnormal, that is painful, but it can help you and your doctor plan the next step, such as another retrieval or considering donor eggs.
- **Supporting single embryo transfer.** Knowing more about an embryo can make families feel more confident transferring one at a time.

## The real limits

This is where I want to be radically honest, because PGT-A is sometimes presented as a guarantee. It is not.

- **It does not make an embryo better.** Testing gives you information. It cannot change the embryo's genetics or improve its chances.
- **It does not guarantee a pregnancy or a healthy baby.** A "normal" result means the sample tested looked normal for chromosome number. Embryos can still fail to implant, and pregnancies can still be lost for reasons unrelated to chromosomes.
- **It does not test for everything.** PGT-A does not screen for most single-gene conditions, and it is not a replacement for prenatal screening during pregnancy. Clinics generally still recommend standard prenatal testing.
- **The sample is only a few cells.** Because cells come from the outer layer, the result may not perfectly match the inner cell mass. False results, in either direction, are possible.
- **There is a small risk to the embryo.** Biopsy is done by skilled embryologists and is generally considered safe, but it is a procedure. Some embryos also do not survive or give a clear result ("no result"), which may mean re-biopsy or a decision to transfer untested.
- **Experts do not fully agree on who benefits most.** Research on PGT-A is ongoing. Professional bodies have cautioned that the evidence for routine use in every patient is not settled. Your doctor can tell you how they view it for someone with your age and history.

## Mosaic embryos

Sometimes results come back "mosaic." That means the sample showed a mix of normal and abnormal cells. It is one of the most confusing results to receive.

A few things to know, in general terms:

- Mosaic embryos were once routinely discarded. Today, many clinics will consider transferring certain mosaic embryos, especially when no euploid (normal) embryos are available, after counselling.
- Some mosaic embryos have resulted in healthy babies. Others are less likely to implant or carry higher risk of loss.
- How a clinic handles mosaic results depends on the type and level of mosaicism, and on clinic policy.

If you receive a mosaic result, ask for a meeting with your doctor or a genetic counsellor before making any decisions. If you are working with a surrogate, she should also be part of that conversation, because the decision to transfer a mosaic embryo affects her too, and your surrogacy agreement may address it.

## What about cost?

PGT-A is usually an extra cost on top of your IVF cycle, and it is not typically covered by provincial funding. Pricing varies a lot from clinic to clinic and depends on how many embryos are tested and which genetics lab is used. Ask your clinic for a written quote that includes biopsy fees, lab fees, and any re-biopsy or freezing charges.

## Is sex selection allowed?

PGT results can show the sex chromosomes. In Canada, the Assisted Human Reproduction Act prohibits choosing an embryo's sex for non-medical reasons. Our FAQ [Can we choose the sex of our baby?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-we-choose-the-sex-of-our-baby) explains more, and your clinic will explain its own policy on what it reports.

## PGT and surrogacy

In surrogacy, many families and clinics lean toward testing, because transferring an embryo involves another person's body and time. Some surrogacy agreements and clinic policies even address whether tested embryos are required or preferred. This is a conversation to have early, with your clinic, your lawyer and your surrogate.

If you are using donor eggs from a younger donor, the conversation may look different, so ask your doctor whether PGT-A is likely to add much in your case.

## Questions to ask your clinic

- Based on my age and history, do you recommend PGT-A? Why?
- What does it cost per embryo, all in?
- What happens with "no result" or mosaic embryos at your clinic?
- Will I meet with a genetic counsellor?
- How long until results, and how does that affect my transfer timeline?
