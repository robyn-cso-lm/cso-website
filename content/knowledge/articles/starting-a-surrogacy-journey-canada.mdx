# What You Should Know Before Starting a Surrogacy Journey in Canada

*A practical guide for intended parents, surrogates, and anyone considering family building through surrogacy.*

## An editorial note from Robyn Price

There are moments in life that change everything.

For some families, it is hearing that carrying a pregnancy may no longer be possible. For others, it is deciding they want to help another family experience parenthood through surrogacy.

Wherever you are in that journey, welcome.

I grew up around surrogacy and family building, and over the years I have had the privilege of walking alongside intended parents, surrogates, donors, fertility clinics, lawyers, counsellors, and many of the professionals who help these journeys move forward.

One thing I have learned is that the strongest journeys do not begin with paperwork or medical appointments.

They begin with understanding.

The purpose of the CSO Knowledge Centre is simple: to provide clear, practical information so people can ask better questions and make informed decisions. Whether or not you choose to work with Canadian Surrogacy Options, I hope these resources help the process feel less overwhelming and more understandable.

— **Robyn Price**

## Surrogacy is more than a medical process

Surrogacy is often explained in medical terms: embryos, screening, medications, transfer and pregnancy.

Those pieces matter, but they are only part of the journey.

Surrogacy is also a relationship built on trust. It involves legal planning, communication, financial preparation, emotional readiness and a team of professionals working together around a shared goal.

In gestational surrogacy, a surrogate carries an embryo created using an egg and sperm from other people. She is not genetically related to the baby she carries.

In Canada, surrogacy operates within an altruistic legal framework. A surrogate cannot be paid a fee simply for carrying a pregnancy, although eligible surrogacy-related expenses may be reimbursed when legal requirements are met. The details matter, and intended parents and surrogates should obtain independent legal advice about their own circumstances.

## Before you begin, prepare before you match

One of the biggest misconceptions about surrogacy is that the journey begins by finding a surrogate.

Usually, the better place to begin is preparation.

Before matching, intended parents should have a working understanding of:

- their fertility clinic and embryo status;
- the legal framework that applies to them;
- realistic timelines;
- the likely financial categories involved;
- what they hope for in a relationship with a surrogate;
- how they will communicate when a journey becomes stressful or uncertain; and
- what professional support they will need along the way.

Women considering becoming surrogates deserve the same opportunity to prepare. That includes understanding clinic screening, legal advice, reimbursements, pregnancy expectations, communication, family support and the practical effect a journey may have on everyday life.

Preparation does not remove every uncertainty. It gives everyone a stronger foundation for handling uncertainty when it appears.

## Build the right professional team

No two journeys are identical, but Canadian surrogacy commonly involves several professionals.

Depending on the circumstances, the team may include:

- a fertility clinic;
- a surrogacy agency or independent coordinator;
- separate lawyers for the intended parent(s) and surrogate;
- a counsellor, psychologist or other mental-health professional;
- family physicians, obstetric providers and other healthcare professionals;
- insurance professionals where applicable; and
- financial or reimbursement administration support.

Each professional has a different role. An agency should not replace legal advice, and a lawyer should not replace medical advice. Clear boundaries between roles help protect everyone involved.

## Understand that timelines are made of phases

There is no single reliable answer to the question, “How long will this take?”

A surrogacy journey typically moves through phases rather than one fixed timeline:

1. education and preparation;
2. profile development and matching;
3. medical and psychological screening;
4. independent legal review and agreements;
5. clinic preparation and embryo transfer;
6. pregnancy; and
7. birth and post-birth legal steps.

Some phases can move quickly. Others depend on clinic scheduling, records, legal review, individual circumstances, medical results and the time it takes to find the right match.

It is reasonable to ask professionals for typical ranges. It is equally important to be cautious of anyone promising an exact outcome or guaranteed timeline.

## Budget for the journey, not just the agency fee

Another common mistake is thinking about surrogacy costs as one number.

The agency or coordination fee is only one category.

A full budget may also need to consider:

- fertility clinic fees;
- medications and embryo transfer costs;
- medical screening;
- legal fees for both parties;
- counselling or psychological assessments;
- surrogate expense reimbursements;
- travel and accommodation;
- insurance-related costs where applicable;
- birth and post-birth legal work; and
- contingency funds for unexpected developments or additional treatment.

Your actual costs depend on your clinic, legal jurisdiction, travel needs, number of transfers and individual journey. The goal should be a realistic plan rather than a perfect prediction.

You can also review CSO's [program options](/programs) and [intended parent resources](/intended-parents) as you begin planning.

## Matching is about compatibility, not speed

Waiting for a match can be one of the hardest parts of surrogacy.

It is natural to want to move quickly, especially when the road to parenthood has already involved years of waiting.

But a match is not simply a person who meets medical criteria.

Intended parents and surrogates need enough alignment around communication, expectations, pregnancy decisions, relationships, practical logistics and the type of journey they hope to have together.

A thoughtful “no” can be healthier than a rushed “yes.”

The right goal is not to create a match at any cost. It is to create the conditions for a relationship that can carry everyone through a very emotional process.

## Have the difficult conversations early

Some of the most important conversations happen before pregnancy.

Topics may include:

- communication frequency;
- attendance at appointments;
- social media and privacy;
- travel;
- medical decision-making;
- pregnancy termination and selective reduction;
- relationships with extended family;
- birth preferences;
- expectations after delivery; and
- how everyone wants the relationship to continue after the journey.

These are deeply personal subjects. They do not need to produce identical opinions on everything, but they do require honest discussion, independent legal advice and a willingness to understand one another.

## Give equal weight to emotional readiness

There is a tendency to focus on whether someone is medically or legally ready for surrogacy.

Emotional readiness matters just as much.

For intended parents, that can mean processing fertility loss or previous treatment before entering a new type of journey.

For prospective surrogates, it can mean considering how pregnancy, appointments, family routines, public questions and relationships with intended parents may affect daily life.

For everyone, it means recognizing that uncertainty is part of assisted reproduction.

Support should not begin only when something goes wrong.

## Questions worth asking before you start

If you are an intended parent, consider asking:

- Are our embryos ready, and what does our clinic require from a surrogate?
- What does our realistic total budget look like?
- What kind of relationship do we hope to have with our surrogate?
- Who will coordinate the different professionals involved?
- What happens if a match does not proceed?
- How will we receive updates while waiting?
- What legal advice do we need for our province, country or cross-border situation?

If you are considering becoming a surrogate, consider asking:

- What does my fertility clinic require?
- Who provides my independent legal advice?
- How are eligible expenses handled?
- Who supports me between major milestones?
- What happens if I am not comfortable with a proposed match?
- How are difficult medical or emotional situations supported?
- What expectations will intended parents have about communication and the relationship?

A good professional should welcome these questions.

## One thing I have learned

Over the years, I have met many families who wanted everything to happen as quickly as possible.

I understand why.

But one thing I have learned is that the strongest surrogacy journeys are rarely built on speed alone.

They are built on trust.

Time spent understanding the process, asking uncomfortable questions, preparing financially and getting to know the people involved is not wasted time. Often, it is what creates the foundation everyone relies on later.
