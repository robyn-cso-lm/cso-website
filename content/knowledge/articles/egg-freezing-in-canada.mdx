Maybe you are in your thirties and the timing just has not been right. Maybe you are focused on school or your career, or you have not met the person you want to build a family with. Maybe a friend froze her eggs and you are wondering if you should too.

Whatever brought you here, you deserve straight answers. I have spent my whole life in fertility, and I went through five egg retrievals myself as a donor, so I know what this process asks of a body. Here is the honest version of elective egg freezing in Canada.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. A fertility doctor can look at your own test results and tell you what egg freezing could realistically mean for you.*

## What egg freezing is

Egg freezing (the medical term is oocyte cryopreservation) means collecting your eggs, freezing them, and storing them for possible use later. If you decide to use them, they are thawed, fertilized with sperm, and the resulting embryo is transferred to your uterus or to a surrogate's.

"Elective" or "social" egg freezing usually means freezing for personal reasons rather than because of an urgent medical treatment. If you are facing cancer treatment or another treatment that could affect fertility, read [fertility preservation before cancer treatment](/knowledge-centre/fertility-preservation-before-cancer-treatment) instead, because the timing and funding are very different.

## How the process works

The first part of egg freezing is the same as the first part of IVF.

1. **Consultation and testing.** Bloodwork, including AMH, and an ultrasound to count follicles. These give a rough sense of how your ovaries may respond, though they do not measure egg quality.
2. **Stimulation.** About 8 to 14 days of hormone injections to grow several follicles at once, with frequent monitoring.
3. **Trigger shot.** A timed injection to finish maturing the eggs.
4. **Retrieval.** A short procedure under sedation, about 36 hours after the trigger.
5. **Freezing.** Mature eggs are frozen using vitrification, a very fast freezing method that has greatly improved how well eggs survive thawing.

From first injection to retrieval is usually about two weeks. Recovery is usually a few days of bloating and cramping. Our [IVF step by step guide](/knowledge-centre/ivf-step-by-step-canada) covers each stage in more detail.

## Why age matters so much

This is the most important thing to understand about egg freezing: **the age at which you freeze your eggs matters far more than the age at which you use them.**

As we get older, both the number of eggs and the proportion of eggs with normal chromosomes decline. That is why fertility doctors generally say that, on average, eggs frozen at a younger age have a better chance of eventually becoming a baby than eggs frozen later. The decline is gradual and then speeds up, especially through the later thirties and into the forties.

I am not going to give you a specific success percentage, because outcomes depend on your age at freezing, how many mature eggs are stored, the clinic's lab, and the sperm used later. Ask your clinic for their own experience with patients your age, and ask them to explain it in terms of the number of eggs you are likely to need.

## How many eggs is "enough"?

There is no magic number. Doctors often talk about freezing a larger number of eggs if you are older, because a smaller share of eggs will become healthy embryos. Some people need more than one retrieval cycle to bank the number their doctor suggests.

It helps to think of it like this. At every step, from thawing, to fertilization, to growing into a blastocyst, to implanting, some eggs will not make it. That is normal. More eggs means more chances.

## What egg freezing can and cannot do

**It can:**
- Give you a chance at a genetic child later, using eggs from a younger version of you.
- Take some pressure off big life decisions.
- Give you options if you later need a surrogate or a sperm donor.

**It cannot:**
- Guarantee a baby.
- Stop your fertility from changing as you age.
- Replace the information a fertility doctor can give about your own body.

Some people who freeze never use their eggs, because they conceive naturally or their plans change. Others use them and it works beautifully. Others thaw them and, sadly, do not end up with a pregnancy. Going in with clear eyes is the kindest thing you can do for your future self.

## Does provincial health insurance cover it?

In general, no. Most provincial health plans do not fund elective egg freezing. Some provinces do fund fertility preservation when it is medically necessary, for example before cancer treatment, and those rules differ by province. Our FAQ on [which provinces help pay for IVF](/knowledge-centre/faqs/which-provinces-fund-ivf-in-canada) and [is IVF covered by provincial health insurance](/knowledge-centre/faqs/is-surrogacy-covered-by-health-insurance-in-canada) explain what is generally covered.

## What it costs

Costs vary a lot between clinics and provinces. Expect separate charges for the cycle itself, the medications (often one of the biggest pieces), and yearly storage. Later, if you use the eggs, there will be more costs for thawing, fertilization, and transfer.

Some workplace benefit plans now include fertility coverage, often with a lifetime maximum. Check yours, and ask your clinic for a full written breakdown before you start. Fertility medications and treatments may also be eligible for the medical expense tax credit, which we explain in [medical expenses and tax credits for surrogacy and IVF](/knowledge-centre/medical-expense-tax-credit-surrogacy-ivf).

## Storage and paperwork

Before freezing, you will sign consent forms about how long your eggs are stored, what happens if you stop paying storage fees, and what happens to them if you die or become unable to decide. Read these carefully. If your plans change, you can usually move eggs to another clinic, though shipping takes coordination. See [shipping embryos and gametes in Canada](/knowledge-centre/shipping-embryos-and-gametes-canada).

## Questions to ask a clinic

- Based on my tests, how many eggs might I get per cycle?
- How many eggs would you suggest I freeze at my age?
- What is your lab's experience thawing and using frozen eggs?
- What are all the costs, including medications and storage?
- What happens to my eggs if I move or change clinics?
