For months, everyone has been focused on the baby. The appointments, the ultrasounds, the countdown to the due date. Then the baby is born, goes home with their parents, and suddenly it is quiet.

This is the part of surrogacy that does not get talked about enough. You just had a baby. Your body is recovering from a pregnancy and birth. Your hormones are shifting. And your heart is processing something big.

You deserve care in this season too. Let me walk you through it.

I grew up in this field, and I have been around surrogates since I was 4 years old. The surrogates who recover best are the ones who plan for the fourth trimester before it arrives, and who let people help.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your OB, midwife or family doctor is the right person for questions about your recovery.*

## Your body is recovering from a birth

It does not matter that the baby went home with someone else. Physically, your postpartum recovery is the same as any other birth. Depending on your delivery, that may include:

- Bleeding and discharge for a number of weeks after birth
- Cramping as your uterus shrinks back down
- Soreness or stitches after a vaginal birth
- Recovery from surgery after a caesarean, which takes longer and needs extra care
- Tiredness, night sweats and hormonal ups and downs
- Breast fullness as your milk comes in, even if you are not planning to breastfeed

Follow your care provider's instructions about rest, lifting, driving and exercise. Go to your postpartum check-ups. And please, do not try to bounce back quickly to prove anything. Rest is part of the job.

## Your milk will likely come in

This surprises some first-time surrogates. Your body does not know the baby went home with their parents, so your milk will usually come in a few days after birth.

You have options, and this is your choice:

- **Letting your milk dry up.** Many surrogates choose this. Your care provider can give you advice on staying comfortable while it happens.
- **Pumping for the baby.** Some surrogates choose to pump breast milk for the family for a period of time. If you are considering this, talk about it with the intended parents well before the birth, and talk about how milk will be stored and delivered. Pumping supplies and related costs should be discussed in advance as part of your reimbursable expenses.
- **Donating milk.** Some surrogates explore donating to a milk bank. Your care provider can tell you about the options in your area.

There is no wrong choice here. Do what is right for your body, your family and your recovery.

## The emotional side

After the birth, many surrogates feel a wave of emotions all at once. Pride. Relief. Joy at seeing the family together. And sometimes a little sadness or emptiness that is hard to explain.

Here is what I want you to hear: that does not mean you regret it, and it does not mean something is wrong. You have been part of something huge for a long time. When it ends, it is natural to feel the space it leaves behind.

On top of that, the postpartum hormone shift is real for everyone who gives birth. Baby blues are common in the first couple of weeks.

Things that help many surrogates:

- **Updates from the family.** A photo or a quick message in the first weeks can mean so much. It is okay to tell the intended parents what kind of contact would feel good to you.
- **Talking about it.** With your partner, a friend, another surrogate or a counsellor. You do not have to carry the feelings alone.
- **Marking the moment.** Some surrogates keep a journal, a photo, a hospital bracelet or a letter from the family. Some celebrate with a special dinner. It is your accomplishment too.

## When to reach out for help

Please contact your care provider right away if you notice:

- Feelings of sadness, anxiety or hopelessness that last more than a couple of weeks, or that get worse
- Trouble sleeping even when you have the chance to rest
- Losing interest in things you normally enjoy
- Thoughts of harming yourself
- Physical warning signs your care provider told you to watch for, such as heavy bleeding, fever, severe pain or signs of infection

Postpartum depression and anxiety can happen to surrogates too. It is not a sign of weakness, and it is very treatable. If you are ever in crisis, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department.

## Expenses during recovery

Recovery takes time, and that time can come with costs. Depending on your agreement, postpartum expenses such as lost wages during recovery, childcare and travel to appointments may be reimbursable with receipts.

Under Canada's *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*, surrogates are reimbursed for receipted expenses, not paid. Keep your receipts and submit them as your agreement describes. Our [surrogacy reimbursements guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-reimbursements-canada-guide) explains how it works.

## Your relationship with the family

Many surrogates stay in touch with their intended parents for years, getting birthday photos and holiday cards. Others have a lighter connection after the birth. Most of the time, this is something you talked about during matching and in your agreement.

If what you expected and what is happening feel different, it is okay to say so gently, or to ask our team for help with that conversation.

## Setting yourself up before the birth

If you are still pregnant and reading this, here is what I suggest you plan now:

- Meals in the freezer or a friend who will organize a meal train
- Help with your kids for the first couple of weeks
- A decision about breast milk, shared with the intended parents
- A plan for how and when the family will send updates
- Time off work that matches your doctor's advice, especially after a caesarean
