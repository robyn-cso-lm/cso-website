Once you have decided donor eggs are part of your path, a new question pops up fast: *fresh or frozen?* You may have heard strong opinions on both sides from friends, online groups or even different clinics. It is easy to feel like you are supposed to already know the answer.

You are not. This is one of those decisions where the right choice depends on your body, your clinic, your timeline and your family plans. Let me walk you through the general trade-offs in plain language so you can have a better conversation with your doctor.

I grew up in this field, I was an egg donor five times, and I founded Little Miracles in 2007. I am not a doctor, though, and this is the part where your clinic's advice matters most.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Please talk with your fertility clinic about what is right for you.*

## What the two options mean

**A fresh donor cycle** means a donor goes through stimulation and egg retrieval for you (or for you and another family, in a [shared-cost cycle](/knowledge-centre/shared-cost-egg-donation-canada)). Her eggs are retrieved and then fertilized with sperm from your partner or a sperm donor. The resulting embryos may be transferred or frozen for later.

**Frozen donor eggs** are eggs that have already been retrieved from a donor and frozen (vitrified) before you are matched with them. When you are ready, the eggs are thawed and fertilized, and the embryos are cultured as usual.

In both cases, the eggs come from a screened donor. The difference is mostly about *when* the eggs are retrieved and *how* the timing works around you.

## Timing and synchronization

This is one of the biggest practical differences.

With a **fresh cycle**, the donor's cycle and your plans have to line up. If the recipient (or a surrogate) is going to have an embryo transferred soon after retrieval, the clinic may need to coordinate cycles. Many clinics now freeze embryos and transfer later, which can take some of that pressure off. Your clinic will tell you how they do it.

With **frozen eggs**, the eggs are already waiting. That can make scheduling simpler, because you are not coordinating around a donor's cycle in real time.

If you have a tight timeline, a surrogate who is ready to go, or a lot of travel involved, timing may matter more to you than anything else. Ask your clinic how each option would fit your calendar.

## Cost and logistics

The costs are structured differently, and it is worth looking at the whole picture rather than one line.

A fresh cycle through an agency involves things like the agency fee, the donor's reimbursed expenses, legal agreements and counselling, plus your clinic's IVF fees. Our [egg donation costs guide](/knowledge-centre/egg-donation-costs-in-canada) lays out the Little Miracles side of a fresh cycle, which is estimated at about $18,950 to $22,700 CAD (higher for non-local donors).

Frozen eggs are usually priced differently, often as a set number of eggs or a package. Before comparing, ask what is included, what is not, and what happens if you do not end up with an embryo you can transfer.

Logistics matter too. A fresh cycle involves a donor travelling to appointments and a retrieval. Frozen eggs may need to be shipped to your clinic, and your clinic will have its own rules about which sources it accepts. Check this before you fall in love with any option.

## Number of eggs and future siblings

With a **fresh cycle**, you generally receive the eggs from that retrieval (or your share, if it is a shared cycle). How many eggs that is varies from donor to donor and cycle to cycle, and nobody can promise a number ahead of time.

With **frozen eggs**, you usually know how many eggs you are getting before you start.

Either way, think about whether you hope to have more than one child from the same donor. Ask how many embryos you might reasonably expect, and whether you could go back to the same donor later. Your clinic is the right place for that conversation, because it depends on so many individual factors.

## Choice and availability

The donor you connect with most may only be available one way. Some donors are available for fresh cycles, and some eggs are available frozen. Depending on what matters most to you, like ethnicity, background, openness to future contact or a particular personal story, availability may help make the decision for you.

I always gently remind families: the donor matters a lot. If you are torn, sometimes it is easier to choose the donor first and let the format follow. Our guide on [how to choose an egg donor](/knowledge-centre/how-to-choose-an-egg-donor) can help with that part.

## What about success?

I know this is the question underneath all the others. I am deliberately not going to quote success numbers here, because they depend on the donor, the lab, the sperm, the recipient or surrogate, and many other factors. General statistics also do not tell you about *your* odds.

Ask your clinic directly:

- How do your results compare between fresh donor cycles and frozen donor eggs?
- What does your lab recommend for someone in my situation?
- How many embryos might I reasonably expect with each option?

A good clinic will give you an honest, individual answer.

## The law is the same either way

Whether eggs are fresh or frozen, Canada's *Assisted Human Reproduction Act* applies. It is illegal to pay a donor for her eggs, and donors can only be reimbursed for receipted expenses. If you are looking at eggs that come from outside Canada, please talk with your clinic and a fertility lawyer about what is permitted before you go any further. You can read more in [Can egg donors be paid in Canada?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-egg-donors-be-paid-in-canada)

## Questions to bring to your clinic

1. Does the clinic work with fresh donor cycles, frozen donor eggs or both?
2. Which would you recommend for my situation, and why?
3. How does timing work with my partner, a sperm donor or a surrogate?
4. What is included in the price of each option, and what is extra?
5. What happens if there are no embryos to transfer?
6. Could I have a future sibling from the same donor?
