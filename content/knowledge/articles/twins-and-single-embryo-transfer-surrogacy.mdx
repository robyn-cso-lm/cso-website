"Can we transfer two embryos and get twins? It would save us a whole second journey."

I hear this question from intended parents all the time, and I completely understand it. Surrogacy is a big investment of time, money and heart. If you always pictured two children, getting them both at once can sound like the dream.

I grew up in this field, and I have watched how practice has changed over the years. Today, in Canada, transferring one embryo at a time is the standard in surrogacy, and I want to explain honestly why, what a twin pregnancy really means for your surrogate and your babies, and how agreements handle the possibility.

*This article is general information, not medical or legal advice. Your fertility clinic decides how many embryos it will transfer, and your lawyer will explain your agreement.*

## What "single embryo transfer" means

Single embryo transfer, sometimes called SET or eSET (elective single embryo transfer), simply means placing one embryo in the uterus at a time. If it does not work, or once you are ready for a sibling, the next embryo is transferred in a separate cycle.

Canadian fertility medicine has moved strongly toward single embryo transfer over the past couple of decades. Professional guidance in Canada encourages it in most situations, and some provincially funded programs and provincial rules limit how many embryos can be transferred. Your clinic will explain its own policy and the rules where you are being treated. For background on how transfers work, see [frozen embryo transfer protocols](/knowledge-centre/frozen-embryo-transfer-protocols) and [embryo grading explained](/knowledge-centre/embryo-grading-explained).

## Why one at a time?

Three big reasons come up again and again.

### 1. Twin pregnancies carry more risk for the surrogate

A twin pregnancy is harder on the body than a single pregnancy. Compared with carrying one baby, carrying twins is generally linked with a higher chance of:

- High blood pressure and preeclampsia
- Gestational diabetes
- Anemia
- More severe nausea and discomfort
- A caesarean birth
- Heavy bleeding after birth
- More time off work, more appointments, and sometimes activity restrictions or hospital stays

Your surrogate is giving you an extraordinary gift. Asking her to take on a higher-risk pregnancy is a serious thing, and many surrogates, and many clinics, are not comfortable with it. For more on how complications are handled in general, read [pregnancy complications in surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/pregnancy-complications-in-surrogacy).

### 2. Twin pregnancies carry more risk for the babies

Twins are much more likely to be born early and smaller than single babies. Early birth can mean a stay in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) and, for some babies, longer-term health challenges. Most twins do wonderfully, but the risks are real, and they are the main reason doctors encourage one baby at a time.

### 3. Better embryos mean you often do not need two

With modern lab techniques, embryos frozen at the blastocyst stage, and options like genetic testing of embryos, one good embryo often has a strong chance on its own. Many clinics find that transferring two does not improve the chance of having a baby enough to justify the extra risk. If you are curious about testing, see [PGT-A embryo testing explained](/knowledge-centre/pgt-a-embryo-testing-explained). Your clinic can talk to you about your own embryos and chances.

## Can twins still happen with one embryo?

Yes, occasionally. A single embryo can split and become identical twins. It is uncommon, but it happens, which is one reason every surrogacy agreement should plan for multiples even when only one embryo is transferred.

## What about saving money?

It is true that two separate pregnancies mean two journeys, with two sets of costs. But a twin pregnancy has costs of its own: more medical monitoring, a higher chance of the surrogate needing time off work earlier, possible extra help at home, a longer hospital stay, and potentially NICU-related travel and accommodation for you. Those reimbursable expenses can add up.

Please do not choose based on money alone. Talk it through with your clinic, and look at the whole picture. Our [surrogacy cost guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-cost-canada-2026) explains the budget for a typical Canadian journey.

## How surrogacy agreements handle multiples

Even with a single embryo transfer, a good surrogacy agreement addresses multiples. Typically it will cover things like:

- **How many embryos can be transferred,** and that the clinic's medical advice guides the decision
- **Whether the surrogate is open to carrying twins,** which should be discussed during matching
- **Reduction and termination,** including what everyone's views are. In Canada, the surrogate always keeps the final say over her own body. Agreements record intentions, and it is essential that everyone's values line up before matching. Your lawyers will explain how this works.
- **Extra reimbursable expenses** that may come with a multiple pregnancy, such as earlier lost wages, housekeeping or childcare help, and maternity clothing

Remember, in Canada surrogates cannot be paid under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*. A twin pregnancy does not change that. What changes is the amount of receipted, pregnancy-related expenses that may be reimbursed. See [what expenses are reimbursed to a surrogate](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-expenses-are-reimbursed-to-a-surrogate).

## If you really want twins

If you have strong feelings about twins, be honest about it from the very beginning:

- Ask your clinic directly whether a double transfer is something it would ever consider in your situation, and why or why not.
- Tell your agency during matching, because many surrogates are open to multiples only if it happens naturally from one embryo, and some are not open at all.
- Talk with your partner about the "two journeys" option. Many families find the second journey is easier, because they already know the process, and sometimes they can work with the same surrogate again.

Matching people whose values line up is one of the most important things an agency does. Read [how surrogate matching works](/knowledge-centre/faqs/how-does-surrogate-matching-work).
