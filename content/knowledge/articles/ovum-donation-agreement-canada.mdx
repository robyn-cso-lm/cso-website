## What an ovum donation agreement is

An ovum donation agreement (often called an "ovum agreement" or "egg donor agreement") is a written contract between the intended parent or parents and the egg donor. It sets out everyone's intentions, rights and responsibilities before the donor starts any medication.

Think of it as putting the important conversations in writing while everyone is calm, clear and on the same page.

## What it typically covers

Every agreement is drafted for the people involved, but most cover topics like these:

- **Intentions and parentage:** that the donor is donating her eggs to help you build your family, and that she does not intend to be a legal parent of any child born
- **Who is responsible for what:** the medical steps the donor agrees to, and your responsibilities as intended parents
- **Expenses:** which donation-related expenses will be reimbursed, and how receipts are handled. Under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*, donors cannot be paid for their eggs, so the agreement deals with reimbursement only
- **Eggs and embryos:** what can be done with the eggs and any resulting embryos, including what happens to any that are not used
- **Confidentiality and contact:** whether identities are shared, and what kind of future contact (if any) everyone is open to, including if the child wants to know more one day
- **Health information:** how the donor will share relevant updates to her medical or family history in the future, if she agrees to
- **Risks and what happens if things change:** for example, if a cycle is cancelled or medical complications come up

A good agreement is clear, fair and specific. It should not leave anyone guessing.

## Why the donor has her own lawyer (ILA)

ILA stands for **independent legal advice.** It means the donor has her own lawyer, separate from yours, who explains the agreement to her and makes sure she understands what she is signing.

I get asked all the time: "Why do we need two lawyers? Isn't that just more cost?" Here is why it matters.

**One lawyer cannot protect both sides.** Your lawyer works for you. The donor deserves someone working for her. Having separate lawyers means each person gets honest advice about their own interests.

**It protects the agreement itself.** When each side has had independent advice, everyone can feel more confident that they understood what they signed and agreed to it freely.

**It protects your child's story.** One day, your child may ask how they came to be. Being able to say "everyone was respected and supported" is a gift.

As someone who has been a donor, I can tell you how much it matters for her to have someone in her corner. Her own lawyer is the person she can ask anything, including the questions she might not feel comfortable asking in front of you.

## When it happens in the process

The agreement is usually signed after you have chosen your donor and screening is underway, and **before the donor begins medication.** Many clinics will not start a donor cycle until the agreement is signed. Your clinic and lawyer will confirm the timing for you.

If you are also working with a surrogate, you will have a separate surrogacy agreement. These are different documents with different purposes. You can read more about that in [What is a surrogacy agreement?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-is-a-surrogacy-agreement)

## Does every province do this the same way?

No. Family law, parentage rules and how birth registration works differ across Canada. That is one of the reasons it matters to work with a lawyer who practices fertility law in your province. They can explain how the agreement fits with the rules where you live and where your child will be born.

Please do not use a template you found online. It might not match the law in your province, and it will not reflect your family's real wishes.

## What about known donors?

If your donor is a sister, cousin or friend, an agreement is just as important, sometimes even more so. Family relationships can make it harder to talk openly about tricky topics, like future contact or what the child will be told. Putting it in writing, with each side having their own lawyer, can actually protect the relationship. Our FAQ [Should we use a known or anonymous egg donor?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/known-donor-or-anonymous-donor) has more on that.

## Questions to ask your lawyer

1. What does this agreement cover, and is anything missing for our situation?
2. How does our province handle parentage for a child born from donor eggs?
3. What happens to unused eggs or embryos?
4. How are future contact and health updates handled?
5. What happens if plans change partway through?
