Whether you are an intended parent with embryos waiting in the freezer, or a surrogate getting ready for your first transfer, the frozen embryo transfer cycle is where things start to feel real. There is a calendar, there are medications, and there is a date circled in red.

Since I was 4 years old, I have been around families and surrogates counting down to transfer day. It is one of my favourite parts of this work. Here is how the cycle usually goes, in plain language.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your clinic will give you a personal protocol, and that protocol is the one to follow.*

## What a frozen embryo transfer is

A frozen embryo transfer (FET) means placing an embryo that was created and frozen in an earlier IVF cycle into the uterus. In surrogacy, almost every transfer is a frozen one, because the embryos are created first and the surrogate's uterus is prepared separately.

The goal of every FET protocol is the same: get the lining of the uterus ready and time the transfer so the embryo arrives when the uterus is most receptive. There are two main ways clinics do this.

## Option 1: Natural cycle FET

In a natural cycle, the clinic works with the body's own ovulation instead of replacing hormones.

- You have monitoring appointments (blood tests and ultrasounds) to watch a follicle grow and the lining thicken.
- The clinic watches for ovulation, sometimes using a trigger shot to time it precisely. This is often called a "modified natural" cycle.
- The transfer is booked a set number of days after ovulation, to match the embryo's age.
- Some clinics add progesterone support after ovulation.

**Possible advantages:** fewer medications, and some people simply prefer it.

**Possible challenges:** it needs fairly regular cycles, timing depends on the body, and the date is less predictable. That can be harder when travel, work schedules, or a surrogate and intended parents in different cities are involved.

## Option 2: Medicated (programmed) FET

In a medicated cycle, the clinic uses hormones to build and time the lining. This is very common in surrogacy because it is predictable.

A typical medicated cycle looks something like this:

1. **Suppression or start of cycle.** Some clinics begin with birth control pills or another medication to quiet the ovaries and help with scheduling. Others start on day 1 to 3 of a period.
2. **Estrogen.** Usually pills, patches, or sometimes injections, taken for roughly two weeks or so. Estrogen thickens the lining.
3. **Lining check.** An ultrasound measures the lining's thickness and pattern. Bloodwork checks hormone levels.
4. **Progesterone.** Once the lining looks ready, progesterone starts. It may be vaginal, by injection, or both. Progesterone changes the lining so it can accept an embryo.
5. **Transfer.** For a blastocyst, transfer is usually scheduled about five or six days after progesterone begins, depending on clinic protocol.
6. **Keep going.** Estrogen and progesterone usually continue until the pregnancy test, and if positive, often for several more weeks until the placenta takes over.

**Possible advantages:** predictable dates, easier coordination, and works well for people with irregular cycles.

**Possible challenges:** more medications, including injections for many people, and side effects like bloating, mood changes, and soreness. ## Lining checks: what the clinic is looking for

The lining check is a big moment, because it decides whether the cycle moves forward. Clinics generally look at:

- **Thickness.** Many clinics like to see the lining reach a certain thickness before starting progesterone. The exact number differs by clinic, and a lining that is a bit thinner is not always a dealbreaker.
- **Pattern.** On ultrasound, a healthy lining often has a "triple line" look.
- **Hormone levels.** To make sure nothing unexpected is happening, such as early ovulation.

If the lining is not ready, the clinic may extend estrogen, change the dose or route, or cancel and restart. A cancelled cycle is frustrating, but it is a safety and success decision, not a failure.

## Some clinics add extra steps

Depending on history, a clinic might suggest:

- A **mock cycle** to see how the lining responds before a real transfer.
- A **saline ultrasound or hysteroscopy** to check the inside of the uterus.
- Other tests if earlier transfers did not work.

These are individual decisions. Your doctor will explain why they recommend anything extra.

## A typical timeline

Every clinic is different, but a medicated FET often looks roughly like this:

| Stage | Roughly when |
|---|---|
| Period starts, baseline check | Day 1 to 3 |
| Estrogen | About two weeks |
| Lining check | Around day 10 to 14 |
| Progesterone starts | Once lining is ready |
| Transfer | About five or six days into progesterone |
| Pregnancy blood test | About 9 to 14 days after transfer |

If suppression medication is used first, add a few weeks at the front.

## Transfer day

Transfer itself is short. A full bladder is often requested to help the ultrasound view. The doctor guides a thin catheter through the cervix and places the embryo. Most people describe it as similar to a Pap test. Afterward, the waiting begins. We have a whole article on [the two-week wait after embryo transfer](/knowledge-centre/the-two-week-wait-after-embryo-transfer).

In surrogacy, intended parents often attend transfer if the clinic and surrogate are comfortable with it. It is one of the most special days of the journey. You might even enjoy our story about the [McDonald's fries transfer day tradition](/knowledge-centre/mcdonalds-fries-transfer-day-tradition).

## Which protocol is right?

That is your doctor's call, based on things like cycle regularity, medical history, past transfers, and clinic experience. It is fine to ask why they recommend one approach over another. If you are a surrogate, it is also fine to ask what the medications involve before you start, so you can plan your life around them.
