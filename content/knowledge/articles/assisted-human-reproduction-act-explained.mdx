If you are looking into surrogacy or egg donation in Canada, you will hear about "the Act" within your first few conversations. Lawyers mention it, clinics mention it, and I mention it on almost every call. It shapes everything about how third-party reproduction works here, from what a surrogate can be reimbursed for to how an egg donor profile is written.

I grew up in this field, and I have watched this law go from brand new to something every family in our program needs to understand. So here is the plain-language version.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Every person in a surrogacy or donation arrangement should have their own independent lawyer, and your lawyer will confirm how the law applies to you.*

## What the AHRA is

The *Assisted Human Reproduction Act* is a federal law passed in 2004. It applies across the whole country, in every province and territory.

Its key prohibitions are criminal law. That matters. Breaking certain parts of the Act is not a contract problem or a regulatory slap on the wrist. It is a federal offence.

What the AHRA does **not** decide is who the legal parents of a baby are. That is provincial law, and it varies from province to province. You can read more in our article on [parentage and birth registration after surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/parentage-and-birth-registration-after-surrogacy).

## The big idea: altruistic, not commercial

The heart of the Act is simple. In Canada, you cannot buy or sell human reproduction.

That means:

- **Surrogates cannot be paid.** Nobody can pay, offer to pay, or advertise payment to a woman to be a surrogate.
- **Egg and sperm donors cannot be paid.** Nobody can buy, offer to buy, or advertise to buy eggs or sperm from a donor.
- **Embryos cannot be bought or sold.** This applies whether the embryo is being sold by a clinic, a family or anyone else.
- **Nobody can accept payment for arranging the services of a surrogate**, or offer or advertise to do so. Your lawyer can explain exactly what this section covers.

The Act also says no one can counsel or help a woman under 21 to become a surrogate, or perform a medical procedure to help her become one if they know or suspect she is under 21.

What surrogates and donors **can** receive is reimbursement of their actual, receipted expenses. That is the whole system in one sentence: helping is allowed, being paid is not, and expenses are covered.

## Other things the Act bans

Most people never come near these, but they are part of the law:

- Human cloning
- Creating embryos for reasons other than having a baby or training people in assisted reproduction
- Keeping an embryo outside the body for more than 14 days of development
- Choosing an embryo's sex, except to prevent, diagnose or treat a sex-linked disorder or disease
- Making genetic changes that could be passed on to future generations
- Creating certain human and animal hybrids or chimeras

The sex-selection rule is the one families ask about most. You can read more in [can we choose the sex of our baby?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-we-choose-the-sex-of-our-baby)

## Consent

The Act requires written consent from donors before their eggs, sperm or embryos are used, under rules set out in regulations. In practice, this is why your clinic has you sign consent forms covering things like what happens to your embryos if you separate, if one of you dies, or if you no longer want to use them. Read those forms slowly. They matter more than most people realize.

## The 2020 reimbursement regulations

For many years, the part of the Act about reimbursing expenses (section 12) was not in force, which left everyone working from guidance and good faith. That changed on June 9, 2020, when section 12 and the *Reimbursement Related to Assisted Human Reproduction Regulations* came into force.

Here is what that means in practice:

- **Only listed categories of expenses can be reimbursed.** The regulations set out the kinds of expenses that qualify for surrogates and donors, such as travel to appointments, medications, counselling, legal advice, maternity clothing, childcare during appointments and similar costs directly related to the surrogacy or donation.
- **Receipts are required.** Every reimbursement must be backed by a receipt. No receipt, no reimbursement.
- **Lost work income has special rules.** A surrogate can be reimbursed for lost income during pregnancy only if a qualified medical practitioner certifies in writing that continuing to work could be a risk to her health, the pregnancy or the baby. The same kind of written medical certification applies for donors.
- **Documentation is expected.** The regulations also expect surrogates and donors to keep records and to provide declarations of the expenses being claimed.

There is no flat "surrogacy fee" in Canada that you can pay in a lump sum and call reimbursement. If someone suggests that, walk away. Our [surrogacy reimbursements guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-reimbursements-canada-guide) and [egg donor expenses guide](/knowledge-centre/egg-donor-expenses-what-can-be-reimbursed) go through the categories in detail.

## Penalties, in general terms

The penalties for breaking the core prohibitions are serious. They can include large fines (into the hundreds of thousands of dollars) and prison time of up to several years, depending on how a case is prosecuted. Prosecutions have been rare, but they have happened, and the possibility is real.

I share this not to scare anyone, but because I want you to understand why good agencies, clinics and lawyers are so careful. When we say "we need the receipt," we are protecting you.

## What it means for you

### If you are an intended parent

- You will pay your surrogate's or donor's eligible expenses, with receipts, usually through a trust or escrow account. See [what is escrow in surrogacy?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-is-escrow-in-surrogacy)
- You cannot offer a bonus, a gift card "thank you" that functions as payment, or anything that looks like payment for carrying your baby or donating eggs.
- Your total budget is driven by expenses, medical costs, legal fees and agency fees, not by a fee paid to the surrogate. Our [surrogacy cost breakdown](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-cost-canada-2026) shows how a typical $80K to $120K CAD budget comes together.
- Your lawyer will make sure your surrogacy agreement is written to fit the Act.

### If you are a surrogate

- You are helping another family, and you should never be out of pocket for doing it.
- Keep every receipt. Truly every one.
- If you need to stop working during the pregnancy, talk to your doctor and your case manager early so the medical certification is in place.
- See [can a surrogate be paid in Canada?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-a-surrogate-be-paid-in-canada) for the short version.

### If you are an egg or sperm donor

- You cannot sell your eggs or sperm, but your eligible expenses can be reimbursed with receipts.
- You will sign detailed consent forms, and you should have your own independent legal advice. Our [ovum donation agreement guide](/knowledge-centre/ovum-donation-agreement-canada) explains how that works.
- See [can egg donors be paid in Canada?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-egg-donors-be-paid-in-canada)
