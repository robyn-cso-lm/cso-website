Maybe your baby is not even here yet, and you are already lying awake wondering how you will explain it. Or maybe your little one is starting to ask where babies come from, and you have that flutter of "is this the moment?"

First, a hug. The fact that you are thinking about this at all tells me your child is going to be very loved.

I grew up in this field, I was an egg donor five times, and I founded Little Miracles in 2007. Over the years I have heard from many families about how they told their children, and the lessons are surprisingly consistent. Here is what I would tell a friend.

*This article is general guidance, not professional counselling. A fertility counsellor can help you plan for your own family.*

## Why telling your child matters

Your child's story belongs to them. Knowing how they came to be, from the start, means it is just a normal part of who they are, rather than a surprise they stumble onto later.

Families who are open from early on often say the same thing: it became ordinary. It was simply part of their family story, like where their parents met or the day they came home from the hospital.

The opposite is also true. Secrets have a way of coming out, through a DNA test, a medical form or an overheard conversation. When a child finds out by accident, the hurt is often less about the egg donation and more about feeling that something was kept from them.

## Start early, start small

You do not need a big sit-down talk. In fact, it usually works better if there never is one.

Many parents begin when their child is a baby or toddler, telling the story out loud long before the child understands it. That gives *you* a chance to practise saying the words until they feel natural.

Something as simple as this is a lovely start:

> "We wanted you so much. We needed help from a kind woman who gave us some of her eggs, so you could grow and come to our family."

As your child grows, you add more detail, a bit at a time, following their questions.

## Use simple, honest words

Kids understand more than we expect, as long as we use words that fit their age.

- **Toddlers and preschoolers:** keep it short and warm. "A kind helper gave us a special part we needed to make you."
- **School-age children:** you can explain that babies grow from an egg and a sperm, and that a donor gave the egg. Answer exactly what they ask, no more.
- **Tweens and teens:** they may want to know more about the donor, about genetics, or about what it means for their identity. Be honest about what you know and what you do not.

Try to avoid words that make the donor sound like the "real mom," or that make the story sound like a problem. You are the parent. The donor is the person who helped.

## Books and stories help

There are children's books written for donor-conceived families, and many parents find them helpful as a starting point. Some families also make their own little book with photos from their journey, or pull out the donor's personal statement from her profile when the child is older. (If you are still choosing a donor, this is one reason I tell families to read the personal statement twice. See [how to choose an egg donor](/knowledge-centre/how-to-choose-an-egg-donor).)

## What about the donor herself?

Your child may one day ask, "Who is she? Can I meet her?"

What you can share depends on what was agreed. Some donors are open to future contact, and some prefer to remain anonymous. This is usually set out in your [ovum donation agreement](/knowledge-centre/ovum-donation-agreement-canada), so it helps to think about it early, before you sign.

If you used a known donor, like a sister or a friend, you will want to talk together about how she will be described to your child and what her role will be. Our FAQ [Should we use a known or anonymous egg donor?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/known-donor-or-anonymous-donor) touches on this too.

## Answering the hard questions

Some days the questions will be easy. Other days they may catch you off guard.

- **"Why couldn't you use your own egg?"** Answer honestly, at their level. You do not have to share every medical detail.
- **"Is she my mom?"** You can say something like, "I am your mom. She is the kind person who helped us have you."
- **"Do I have brothers or sisters?"** If you know of other children from the same donor, or if you did a [shared-cost cycle](/knowledge-centre/shared-cost-egg-donation-canada), think ahead about how you will answer.

If you do not know an answer, it is okay to say "I don't know, but we can find out together" or "That is a great question. Let me think about it and we will talk tonight."

## Taking care of your own feelings

Here is the part people do not say enough. Sometimes the hardest part is not the child's reaction. It is ours.

You might feel grief about not using your own eggs, or worry that your child will love you less. Those feelings are real and they deserve care. Talking with a fertility counsellor, your partner or other families who have used donor eggs can help you work through them, so you can tell your child's story with confidence.

Your child will take their cue from you. If you speak about it with warmth and pride, that is how they will feel about it too.

## You are already doing this right

There is no perfect script. What matters most is that the door is open, the tone is loving and your child knows they can always ask.
