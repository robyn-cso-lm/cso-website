You have been matched, the agreement is signed, and now a box of medications (or a long list from the clinic pharmacy) is sitting on your kitchen counter. If you are feeling a little overwhelmed, that is completely normal. Almost every surrogate I have worked with felt that way the first time she opened her calendar from the clinic.

I grew up in this field, and I have also been on the other side of the needle as an egg donor five times. So I want to walk you through what a typical surrogate protocol looks like, in plain words, so that calendar makes a lot more sense.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Every clinic uses its own protocol, and your clinic will tell you exactly what to take, when, and how. If anything here differs from your clinic's instructions, follow your clinic.*

## Why a surrogate takes medications at all

In gestational surrogacy, the embryo was created from the intended parents' eggs and sperm, or from donor eggs or sperm. Your body did not make it. That means your uterus needs a little help getting ready at exactly the right time to welcome it.

The goal of the protocol is simple: build a thick, healthy uterine lining and then support it with the right hormones so an embryo has the best possible place to implant. Most transfers in Canada today use frozen embryos, which gives the clinic a lot of control over the timing. You can read more about the different approaches in [frozen embryo transfer protocols](/knowledge-centre/frozen-embryo-transfer-protocols).

## The usual phases of a protocol

Clinics vary, but most medicated transfer cycles follow a similar shape.

### 1. Getting your cycle lined up

Some clinics start with birth control pills or another medication to quiet your natural cycle and line up the timing. Others start from the first day of your period. You may be asked to call the clinic on "day 1" so they can build your calendar.

### 2. Estrogen to build the lining

Next comes estrogen. It may be pills, patches, vaginal tablets, or sometimes injections, depending on your clinic. Estrogen thickens the lining of your uterus over roughly two to three weeks.

During this time you will usually have monitoring appointments: bloodwork and an internal ultrasound to measure your lining. If you live far from the clinic, monitoring can often happen at a clinic closer to home (sometimes called satellite monitoring), with results sent over.

### 3. Progesterone to prepare for transfer

Once your lining looks good, progesterone is added. Progesterone changes the lining so it is ready to accept an embryo, and the transfer date is timed from the day progesterone starts. This is why your start date matters so much and why your clinic may be very precise about the time of day you take it.

Progesterone can come in a few forms:

- **Progesterone in oil (PIO):** an intramuscular injection, usually given in the upper outer area of the buttock. This is the one surrogates talk about the most.
- **Vaginal progesterone:** suppositories, gels or inserts.
- **Other forms:** some clinics use a combination, or a different injectable form.

Many clinics use more than one type at the same time.

### 4. Transfer day

The embryo transfer itself is quick and usually feels a lot like a Pap test. You will often be asked to arrive with a comfortably full bladder so the ultrasound picture is clearer. Then you go home and rest, and keep taking every medication exactly as prescribed.

### 5. After transfer and early pregnancy

You keep taking estrogen and progesterone after the transfer. Your clinic will do a blood pregnancy test (a beta hCG test) about one and a half to two weeks later. If you are pregnant, the medications usually continue for several more weeks while the placenta takes over hormone production. Many clinics wean these medications somewhere around the end of the first trimester, often around 10 to 12 weeks, but your clinic sets this timing. Never stop or reduce a medication on your own.

The waiting part is its own adventure. We have a whole article on [the two-week wait after embryo transfer](/knowledge-centre/the-two-week-wait-after-embryo-transfer).

## Other medications you might see

Depending on your history and your clinic, your calendar might also include:

- A daily low-dose aspirin or a prenatal vitamin with folic acid
- An antibiotic or a steroid around transfer day
- A blood thinner for some people with certain medical histories
- Something to help you relax on transfer day

If you do not understand why something is on your list, ask. A good clinic nurse would much rather answer a question than have you guess.

## Common side effects

Everyone is different, and many surrogates feel mostly fine. Things people commonly mention:

- **From estrogen:** headaches, breast tenderness, bloating, mood swings, feeling a bit emotional
- **From progesterone:** tiredness, bloating, breast tenderness, constipation, and symptoms that feel a lot like early pregnancy (which makes the two-week wait extra confusing)
- **From PIO injections:** soreness, lumps or bruising at the injection site
- **From vaginal progesterone:** discharge and irritation

Call your clinic right away if you have signs of an allergic reaction, a hot, red, very painful injection site, severe pain, heavy bleeding, chest pain, shortness of breath, or pain and swelling in one leg. Trust your gut. If something feels wrong, call.

## Tips from surrogates who have done it

These are the practical things surrogates tell me helped them most. Run them by your clinic nurse first, since some clinics have their own preferences.

**For PIO injections:**

- Warm the vial a bit first (in your bra or a pocket for a few minutes), since warmer oil tends to go in more easily.
- Ice or a numbing cream on the spot before, if your clinic says it is okay.
- Inject slowly, then massage the area afterward.
- Apply a heating pad after and go for a short walk to help the oil spread.
- Alternate sides every day.
- Ask your nurse to mark the right spot on you, or teach your partner, so you are not guessing.

**For staying on schedule:**

- Set phone alarms for every dose, with the medication name in the alarm.
- Use a printed calendar or app and check off each dose.
- Keep a small travel kit with your medications if you will be away from home.
- Refill early. Pharmacies sometimes need a few days to order fertility medications.

**For your sanity:**

- Ask your partner or a friend to be your injection buddy. Many partners end up being great at this. Our [guide for partners](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-guide-for-partners) talks about how they can help.
- Tell the intended parents what kind of updates you are comfortable sharing during this phase.

## Who pays for the medications?

In Canada, surrogates cannot be paid, but they also should not be out of pocket. Transfer medications are an expense of the surrogacy, and they are typically covered by the intended parents directly or through reimbursement from an escrow or trust account, as set out in your surrogacy agreement. Keep every receipt. Some surrogates have workplace drug coverage, and whether you use it is something to talk through before the agreement is signed. Your lawyer can explain how your agreement handles it.
