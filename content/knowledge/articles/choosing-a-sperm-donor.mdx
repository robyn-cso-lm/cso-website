You open a sperm bank catalogue and suddenly you are scrolling through hundreds of strangers. Height, eye colour, university major, favourite food, a baby photo, an audio interview. It can feel thrilling and a little surreal at the same time. How are you supposed to choose half of your future child's genes from a list?

I have spent my whole life in this field (I grew up in it, since I was 4 years old), and I have sat with a lot of families staring at donor profiles. Here is what I have learned about choosing well, and about the things that matter more than people expect.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your fertility clinic and a genetic counsellor can help you with your own results and choices.*

## Start with your must-haves, not the catalogue

Before you look at a single profile, sit down (with your partner, if you have one) and write two short lists:

- **Must-haves:** the things you will not compromise on. For many families that is ID release, a clean carrier screening match with the person providing the eggs, and a certain ethnic or cultural background.
- **Nice-to-haves:** things that would be lovely but are not deal breakers, like height, hair colour or education.

This protects you from falling for a charming essay and forgetting the basics. It also helps when your first choice sells out, which happens more than you would think.

## Check what your clinic can actually accept

Most donor sperm used in Canada is imported, usually from large US banks. Not every bank ships to Canada, and not every donor in a catalogue meets Canadian safety requirements. Ask your clinic early:

- Which banks do you work with?
- How do I know a donor is eligible for use in Canada?
- What do you charge to receive and store the vials?

If you want the bigger picture on Health Canada rules and quarantine, read [sperm donation in Canada](/knowledge-centre/sperm-donation-in-canada).

## What is in a donor profile

Profiles vary by bank, but you will usually see:

- **Physical traits:** height, weight, eye and hair colour, skin tone, blood type
- **Background:** ethnicity, ancestry, sometimes religion
- **Education and work**
- **Personal history:** interests, personality, a written essay or interview
- **Family medical history:** usually going back to grandparents
- **Test results:** infectious disease testing and genetic carrier screening
- **Extras:** childhood photos, adult photos in some cases, audio interviews, staff impressions

A few tips:

- **Read the medical history slowly.** Look at family patterns, not just the donor himself. If something worries you, ask the bank for more detail, or ask a genetic counsellor what it means.
- **Staff impressions are useful.** They are often the only description written by someone who actually met him.
- **Do not over-weight the essay.** A great writer is not the same as a great genetic match. Lots of wonderful donors are just not wordy.

## ID release vs anonymous donors

This is one of the biggest decisions you will make, and it is really a decision about your future child.

- **ID release (also called open identity or identity release):** the donor has agreed that the child can request his identifying information, usually once they reach adulthood (often 18). Some banks also offer limited contact options.
- **Anonymous (non-ID release):** the donor has not agreed to share identifying information through the bank.

Here is the radical honesty part: in a world of consumer DNA tests, true anonymity is very hard to guarantee. A donor-conceived adult can spit in a tube and find genetic relatives, sometimes the donor himself. I walk through that in [donor-conceived people, their rights and DNA tests](/knowledge-centre/donor-conceived-people-rights-and-dna-tests).

Many donor-conceived adults say they wish they had been given the option to know who their donor was. For that reason, a lot of families now choose ID release donors whenever they can. It does not force your child to do anything. It just keeps a door open for them.

## Family limits and donor siblings

A single donor can be used by many families. That means your child may have genetic half siblings, sometimes called donor siblings, in other homes.

Things to know:

- **Canada does not have a national legal limit** on how many families can use the same sperm donor. Limits are usually set by the bank, and they vary.
- **Ask every bank:** What is your family limit for this donor? Is it worldwide or per country? How do you track births? Do families report back?
- **Some banks have sibling registries** where families can choose to connect. There are also independent registries.
- **If you want siblings for your own child from the same donor,** ask about buying and storing extra vials now. Popular donors sell out, and once a donor reaches his limit, his vials may no longer be sold.

Smaller family limits mean fewer half siblings, which many families and donor-conceived adults see as a positive.

## Genetic carrier screening

Most reputable banks screen donors for a panel of recessive genetic conditions. Being a carrier usually means a person is healthy but carries one copy of a gene change. If both genetic parents carry a change in the same gene, there can be a chance of the child inheriting the condition.

What that means for you:

- **The person providing the eggs should usually be screened too,** using a panel that overlaps well with the donor's. That might be you, your partner, or an egg donor.
- **Compare like with like.** Different banks and labs test different numbers of conditions. A genetic counsellor can tell you whether the two panels line up.
- **A carrier result is not automatically a "no".** It depends on what the other genetic parent carries. That is exactly why matching the screening matters.
- **No screening is perfect.** Carrier screening lowers risk, it does not eliminate it.

If you are also using an egg donor, our guides to [egg donor screening](/knowledge-centre/egg-donor-screening-explained) and [how to choose an egg donor](/knowledge-centre/how-to-choose-an-egg-donor) cover the other half of the picture.

## Practical things people forget

- **Vial type.** Some vials are prepared for IUI and some for IVF. Ask your clinic which you need before you buy.
- **How many vials.** Your clinic can suggest how many to buy for your treatment plan. Buying too few and then losing the donor is a common heartbreak.
- **Storage.** You will usually pay storage fees for extra vials.
- **CMV status.** Some clinics care about whether the donor and recipient have been exposed to cytomegalovirus. Ask if it matters for you.
- **Return or buy-back policies.** Policies on unused vials vary. Read them before you buy.

## Trust yourself

After the checklists and the screening reports, some families just know. They read a line in a staff impression or hear a laugh in an audio clip and something clicks. That is okay. As long as your must-haves are met, your gut is allowed to have a vote.
