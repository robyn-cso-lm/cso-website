If you are reading this, you may have just heard the words "scar tissue in your uterus" for the first time. Maybe your periods became very light or stopped after a D&C. Maybe transfer after transfer has not worked, and someone finally looked inside your uterus and found the answer. Either way, you are probably tired, confused and wondering whether you will ever carry a baby.

Let me walk you through what Asherman's syndrome is, how it is usually treated, and when families end up talking about surrogacy. Then you can go into your next appointment with better questions.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your fertility specialist will confirm what applies to you.*

## What Asherman's syndrome is

Asherman's syndrome means there is scar tissue (doctors call these adhesions) inside the uterus. The scar tissue can be thin and filmy, or thick and dense. It can cover a small area, or it can make the walls of the uterus stick together.

The lining of the uterus, the endometrium, is where an embryo implants and grows. When scarring damages that lining or takes up the space inside the uterus, it can make it hard for an embryo to implant, or hard for a pregnancy to continue.

## How it usually happens

Asherman's is most often linked to some kind of procedure or injury inside the uterus. Common causes include:

- A D&C (dilation and curettage), especially one done after a miscarriage, a retained placenta, or bleeding after birth
- Other surgery on the uterus, such as fibroid removal
- Some infections of the uterine lining

You did not cause this. Many women had a procedure that was necessary at the time and had no way of knowing scarring could follow.

## Signs and diagnosis

Some women notice very light periods, no periods, or pelvic pain around the time a period should come. Others have no symptoms at all and only find out after repeated failed transfers or pregnancy losses.

Diagnosis is usually made with:

- **Hysteroscopy**, where a thin camera is passed through the cervix to look directly inside the uterus. This is considered the most reliable way to see adhesions.
- **Saline ultrasound (sonohysterogram)** or other imaging, which can suggest scarring and help decide whether a hysteroscopy is needed.

## Treatment comes first for most people

For many women, Asherman's can be treated, and a pregnancy is still possible. Treatment usually involves:

- **Hysteroscopic surgery** to carefully cut and remove the scar tissue. Some women need more than one procedure.
- **Steps to stop the scarring from coming back**, which may include hormone treatment to help the lining regrow, or a temporary device placed in the uterus to keep the walls apart while it heals.
- **Follow up checks** to see whether the uterus has stayed open and the lining is growing well.

Outcomes depend a lot on how severe the scarring was to begin with. Mild cases often respond well. Severe cases are harder, and it can take time to find out how your uterus responds. Your specialist is the best person to tell you where you sit.

It is also worth knowing that a pregnancy after Asherman's can carry some extra risks, including problems with how the placenta attaches. Your doctor will talk with you about this if you are trying to carry yourself.

## When surrogacy becomes part of the conversation

Doctors may start to talk about a gestational surrogate when:

- The scarring is severe, or keeps coming back after treatment
- The lining stays very thin and does not respond to treatment
- You have had several good quality embryos transferred without a pregnancy, and the uterus is the most likely reason
- You have had repeated losses linked to the uterus
- A pregnancy would carry high risks to your own health

I want to be clear about something: being offered surrogacy as an option is not the same as being told you have failed. It is a medical tool, the same way IVF is. Some women choose to keep trying treatment for a while longer, and some feel relief at being able to step off that road. Both are valid.

## Your eggs and embryos still matter

Asherman's affects the uterus, not the ovaries. That means your eggs usually are not affected by it. If you already have frozen embryos, those can typically be transferred to a gestational surrogate. If you do not, you can usually do IVF with your own eggs, and the embryos are transferred to her.

In gestational surrogacy, the surrogate has no genetic connection to the baby. ## The emotional weight of this one

Asherman's often comes after a loss. Many women develop it after a miscarriage, a stillbirth, or a hard delivery. So the grief can stack: the loss itself, then the scarring, then the news that carrying again may not be possible.

If that is where you are, please be gentle with yourself. It is okay to need a pause before deciding anything. A counsellor with fertility experience can help you sort through it. Our article on [infertility grief and mental health](/knowledge-centre/infertility-grief-and-mental-health) talks about support options in Canada.

## How surrogacy works in Canada

In Canada, surrogacy is altruistic. The *Assisted Human Reproduction Act* does not allow surrogates to be paid. They can be reimbursed for receipted, pregnancy-related expenses, which the intended parents cover. Parentage after birth is handled under provincial law.

A typical Canadian surrogacy journey costs about $80K to $120K CAD all in. See our [surrogacy cost breakdown](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-cost-canada-2026) and our realistic [surrogacy timeline](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-timeline-canada).

## Questions to bring to your specialist

- How severe is my scarring, and what are the chances treatment will work for me?
- How many treatments would you recommend before considering another path?
- How thick is my lining getting, and is it responding to hormones?
- If I carry a pregnancy, what extra risks should I know about?
- If we move to surrogacy, can we use the embryos we already have?
