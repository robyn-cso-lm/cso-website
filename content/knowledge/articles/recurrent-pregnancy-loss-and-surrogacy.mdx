If you have lost more than one pregnancy, I am so sorry. Every one of those losses was a baby you were already imagining, and the fear that comes with each new positive test is something no one should have to carry.

At some point, many families in your position ask me: "Should we just use a surrogate?" It is a fair question. The honest answer is: sometimes surrogacy is exactly the right next step, and sometimes it will not solve the problem at all. The difference comes down to *why* the losses are happening. This article will help you understand that.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your doctor will confirm what applies to you.*

## What "recurrent pregnancy loss" means

Recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) generally means two or more pregnancy losses. Some doctors and guidelines use three as the starting point. Either way, once you have had more than one loss, it is reasonable to ask for a proper workup rather than being told to "just keep trying."

You deserve answers, or at least a real search for them.

## Testing usually comes first

Before anyone recommends surrogacy, a fertility specialist or a recurrent loss clinic will usually look for causes. The workup commonly includes some combination of:

- **A look at the uterus.** Ultrasound, saline ultrasound or hysteroscopy can find things like a uterine septum, fibroids, polyps or scarring (see [Asherman's syndrome and surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/ashermans-syndrome-and-surrogacy)).
- **Blood tests for clotting and immune conditions,** such as antiphospholipid syndrome.
- **Hormone and metabolic checks,** often including thyroid function and blood sugar.
- **Chromosome testing of both partners,** to look for balanced translocations that can lead to losses.
- **Testing of pregnancy tissue,** if it was available from a loss, to see whether the pregnancy had a chromosomal problem.

Your clinic may add or skip tests depending on your history. Ask what was checked and what was not, and keep copies of your results.

It is also important to know that for a large share of couples, testing does not find a clear cause. That is incredibly frustrating. It is also not the end of the road, and many couples with unexplained losses go on to have a baby.

## When surrogacy can help

Surrogacy addresses one thing: the uterus and the pregnancy environment. So it tends to be considered when:

- **A uterine problem has been found** that cannot be fixed, or keeps coming back after treatment
- **Carrying a pregnancy is risky for your health,** for example because of a heart, kidney or autoimmune condition
- **Chromosomally normal embryos have been lost,** suggesting the issue is not the embryo itself
- **You have been through treatment for a known cause** and the losses have continued
- **Your doctor feels other options have been reasonably explored,** and you are emotionally ready to stop carrying

## When surrogacy may not help

This is the part I wish more people heard early. The most common reason for a single miscarriage is a chromosomal problem in the embryo. If repeated losses are mainly being driven by the embryos, for example because of egg quality, sperm factors, or a balanced translocation in one parent, then moving those same embryos into a surrogate's uterus may not change the outcome.

In those situations, your doctor may talk about other options instead of, or alongside, surrogacy:

- **Genetic testing of embryos** before transfer. Read [PGT-A embryo testing explained](/knowledge-centre/pgt-a-embryo-testing-explained) for how this works and its limits.
- **Donor eggs or donor sperm**, if gamete quality is the issue. See [do I need an egg donor, a surrogate, or both?](/knowledge-centre/do-i-need-an-egg-donor-a-surrogate-or-both)
- **Treatment for a specific condition**, like a clotting disorder, that may let you carry safely.

I would much rather you spend a few extra months getting good answers than start a surrogacy journey that does not address the real cause. A good agency will tell you that too.

## Questions to ask before deciding

- What did my testing show, and what was not tested?
- Do you think the losses are more likely about the uterus, the embryos, or something else?
- Have any of my lost pregnancies had chromosome testing, and what did it show?
- Would testing our embryos before transfer change your advice?
- If we used a surrogate with the same embryos, would you expect a different result?

## The grief that comes with repeated loss

Recurrent loss is a particular kind of grief. It is a series of losses, each layered on the last, often with very little public acknowledgment. Many people do not know how many babies you have lost. Some do not count early losses as "real." You know they were real.

You may also notice:

- **Fear that steals the joy** from every positive test
- **Strain in your relationship,** especially if you and your partner grieve differently
- **Guilt or blame,** even though recurrent loss is not caused by anything you did
- **Complicated feelings about surrogacy,** including grief for carrying, and relief at handing the risk to someone else

All of that is normal. I always say: to grieve is to know love. Every loss you are carrying is proof of how much you already love the family you are trying to build.

Please do not go through this alone. A counsellor with fertility and pregnancy loss experience can help, and so can talking to others who have walked this road. Read [infertility grief and mental health](/knowledge-centre/infertility-grief-and-mental-health) for support options in Canada, and [when surrogacy does not go as planned](/knowledge-centre/when-surrogacy-does-not-go-as-planned) if you are worried about loss during a surrogacy journey too.

## If you choose surrogacy

In Canada, surrogacy is altruistic. Under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*, surrogates cannot be paid. They are reimbursed for receipted, pregnancy-related expenses, paid by the intended parents. A typical Canadian surrogacy journey costs about $80K to $120K CAD all in. Our [surrogacy cost breakdown](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-cost-canada-2026) walks through where that money goes.

A surrogacy pregnancy can still end in loss. It is less common when the cause was uterine, but no one can promise a pregnancy will continue. Going in with your eyes open helps.
