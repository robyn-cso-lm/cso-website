Your embryos are in one place, and your journey needs them somewhere else. Maybe your surrogate lives in another province and will use a clinic closer to home. Maybe you did IVF abroad and want to bring your embryos to Canada. Maybe you are moving, or switching clinics.

It is nerve-racking to think about your embryos travelling. I understand. These are not just samples. They are your hopes. The good news is that moving frozen embryos, eggs and sperm is a routine part of fertility care, and it happens safely all the time when it is planned well.

*This article is general information, not medical or legal advice. Your sending and receiving clinics will confirm their exact requirements, and a lawyer can advise on cross-border or legal questions.*

## Why families move embryos and gametes

Common reasons include:

- **Surrogate in another city or province.** Many intended parents create embryos at one clinic, then ship them to the clinic where the transfer will happen.
- **Switching clinics** for personal, medical or practical reasons.
- **International families** bringing embryos into Canada for a Canadian surrogacy, or moving them out of Canada.
- **Donor eggs or sperm** coming from a bank or clinic elsewhere.
- **Moving house** and wanting your frozen material closer to you.

If your surrogate lives near you, you may never need to ship at all. See [does a surrogate need to live near the intended parents?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/does-a-surrogate-need-to-live-near-the-intended-parents)

## How the shipping itself works

Frozen embryos, eggs and sperm are stored in liquid nitrogen at extremely cold temperatures, around minus 196 degrees Celsius. To move them, clinics use a **cryoshipper**, sometimes called a dry shipper.

In simple terms:

- A cryoshipper is a specially designed container that absorbs liquid nitrogen into its walls, so it stays deeply frozen without loose liquid sloshing around.
- It keeps contents frozen for a set number of days, which gives a safety margin if a shipment is delayed.
- Many shippers include a temperature data logger, so the receiving clinic can confirm the contents stayed frozen the whole way.
- Specialized courier companies that handle reproductive tissue usually do the transport. Some clinics have their own preferred couriers.

Your clinics coordinate the actual handoff. The sending lab packs the straws or devices, and the receiving lab checks them in and moves them into their own storage tanks.

Costs vary widely depending on distance, the courier and whether the shipment crosses a border, so ask your clinics and the courier for a quote. Some families also ask about shipment insurance.

## A compatibility check before anything moves

This is the step people do not know to ask about. Before you ship, the receiving lab should confirm it can thaw what you are sending.

- **Freezing method.** Most embryos and eggs today are frozen by vitrification, a fast-freezing method. Older material may have been slow-frozen. The receiving lab needs to know which.
- **Device type.** Different labs use different freezing devices and warming kits. The receiving lab should confirm it can work with yours.
- **Embryo details.** The sending clinic should provide a record of each embryo, such as its day of development, grade and any genetic testing results. See [embryo grading explained](/knowledge-centre/embryo-grading-explained) and [PGT-A embryo testing explained](/knowledge-centre/pgt-a-embryo-testing-explained).

Ask both labs to talk to each other directly. A short lab-to-lab conversation prevents most problems.

## The paperwork

Expect a stack of forms. Typically:

- **Release or transfer consent.** Both clinics will want written consent from the people who own the embryos or gametes. If embryos were created by a couple, both partners usually need to sign.
- **Original consent forms.** Canadian law requires written consent for the use of donor material and embryos. The receiving clinic will want copies.
- **Infectious disease testing records** for the people who provided the eggs and sperm, and sometimes repeat testing depending on the clinic's rules.
- **Donor documents** if donor eggs or sperm were used, including screening records.
- **Shipping agreement** with the courier.
- **Storage agreement** with the new clinic.

Start this early. It is very common for the paperwork, not the shipping, to be what delays a transfer.

## Moving within Canada

Shipping between Canadian clinics, including between provinces, is the most straightforward case. The main steps are:

1. Tell your new clinic you want to transfer material in, and ask for their intake requirements.
2. Ask your current clinic for their release forms and fees.
3. Confirm compatibility between the two labs.
4. Book the courier once both clinics are ready.
5. Get written confirmation when your material has arrived safely.

Plan for this to take a few weeks from start to finish, sometimes longer.

## Bringing material into Canada

Importing is more involved, and the rules depend on what you are bringing.

**Donor eggs and sperm.** Health Canada regulates the safety of donor sperm and eggs used in assisted reproduction under the *Safety of Sperm and Ova Regulations*. In general terms, donor material has to be screened, tested, processed and handled according to Canadian requirements before it can be imported or used here. Donor material that does not meet those requirements may, in limited situations, be accessed through a special process involving your physician. Your Canadian clinic will tell you whether a particular donor's material can be brought in. Our article on [choosing a sperm donor](/knowledge-centre/choosing-a-sperm-donor) covers what to ask a bank.

**Embryos.** Embryos created with your own eggs and sperm are usually simpler, but your receiving clinic will still have requirements around testing, consent and documentation. If donor eggs or sperm were used to create the embryos, expect extra questions about the donor's screening and consent.

**At the border.** Shipments must go through customs, and the courier will usually handle the declarations. Delays at the border are one reason the shipper's holding time matters.

**The law still applies.** Canada's *Assisted Human Reproduction Act* bans buying and selling eggs, sperm and embryos. If you used paid donors abroad, talk to a Canadian lawyer and your clinic before you ship anything, so you understand how Canadian rules apply to your situation. See [the AHRA explained](/knowledge-centre/assisted-human-reproduction-act-explained).

## Sending material out of Canada

If you are taking embryos or gametes from Canada to another country, the destination country's rules apply on arrival. Some countries are strict about donor screening, consent wording or who can import. Ask the receiving clinic abroad for their checklist before you start, and expect international shipments to take longer and cost more.

International families doing surrogacy here should also read [surrogacy for international intended parents](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-for-international-intended-parents-canada).

## Can we carry them ourselves?

Some clinics allow patients to hand-carry a cryoshipper, and many do not. Even where it is allowed, airlines, security screening and customs can add risk. Most families find a specialized courier is worth it. Ask your clinic what they allow.

## What can go wrong, honestly

Shipping problems are uncommon, but they can happen: delays, damaged containers, missing paperwork, or a receiving lab that cannot thaw a particular device. That is why the planning steps above matter. If anything worries you, ask your clinic how they handle a delayed or compromised shipment before you sign the release.
