Maybe you are a single woman ready to start a family on your own. Maybe you are a lesbian couple deciding who will carry. Maybe you and your partner just got news from a semen analysis that changed your plans. Or maybe you are a single dad or a gay couple who will need donor eggs, and you are reading everything you can about third-party reproduction.

Whatever brought you here, donor sperm builds a lot of Canadian families, and it is one of the least explained parts of this world. Let me walk you through it plainly.

*This article is general information, not medical or legal advice. Your fertility clinic and your lawyer will confirm what applies to you.*

## The short version

- Donor sperm in Canada is regulated federally by Health Canada for safety, and by the Assisted Human Reproduction Act (AHRA) for payment and consent.
- Most donor sperm used in Canadian clinics is imported, usually from large banks in the United States, through a Canadian clinic or distributor.
- Donor sperm is typically frozen, quarantined and the donor retested before it can be released for use.
- It is a crime in Canada to pay a donor for sperm. Donors can only be reimbursed for receipted expenses.
- Costs vary a lot depending on the bank, the type of donor, shipping, and whether you use it for insemination (IUI) or IVF.

## The two federal laws that shape everything

### Health Canada's safety rules

Health Canada regulates donor sperm under the Safety of Sperm and Ova Regulations, which came into force in 2020 under the AHRA. In plain words, these rules are about making sure donor sperm is as safe as reasonably possible before it is used to make a baby.

That generally means:

- **Donor screening:** a detailed medical and social history, with questions about health, family history and risk factors for infectious disease.
- **Donor testing:** lab tests for infectious diseases such as HIV, hepatitis and certain sexually transmitted infections.
- **Quarantine and retesting:** donor sperm is usually frozen and held for a period of time (commonly at least six months), then the donor is tested again before the sperm is released. This window exists because some infections take time to show up on a test.
- **Registration and record keeping:** the establishments that process, import or distribute donor sperm have to be registered with Health Canada and keep records that allow a donation to be traced.

There is an important exception. If the sperm comes from your spouse or sexual partner, it is generally not treated as "donor" sperm under these rules. There is also a pathway called exceptional distribution, which in some situations lets a doctor use sperm that has not met every requirement, with the recipient's informed consent. Your clinic will explain whether either of these applies to you.

### The Assisted Human Reproduction Act

The AHRA is a federal criminal law. For sperm donation, the parts that matter most are:

- **No buying sperm from a donor.** It is illegal to purchase, or offer to purchase, sperm from a donor or from someone acting on the donor's behalf. That also means it is illegal to advertise to buy it.
- **Reimbursement only.** A donor can be reimbursed for receipted expenses directly related to donating, in line with the federal reimbursement regulations. That is not a fee and it is not compensation.
- **Consent.** Donors must give written informed consent to how their sperm will be used.

This is the same altruistic model that applies to egg donors and surrogates in Canada. If you want the bigger picture, read [the legal framework for surrogacy in Canada](/knowledge-centre/legal-framework-canada) and [the Assisted Human Reproduction Act explained](/knowledge-centre/assisted-human-reproduction-act-explained).

## Why so much donor sperm is imported

Because Canadian donors cannot be paid, there are relatively few Canadian sperm donors. Most Canadian families who use an unknown donor end up choosing from a catalogue at a large sperm bank, very often in the United States, and the sperm is imported through a Canadian clinic or licensed distributor.

A few practical things to know:

- **Not every bank ships to Canada.** The bank has to be able to meet Canadian safety rules, and the sperm has to come in through an establishment registered with Health Canada. Ask your clinic which banks it works with before you fall in love with a donor.
- **Not every donor is eligible for Canada.** Some donors in a bank's catalogue are marked as available for Canadian clients and some are not, depending on whether their screening and testing meets Canadian requirements.
- **Your clinic usually handles the order.** In many cases you pick the donor and buy the vials, and the bank ships to your clinic, not to your home.
- **Inventory changes.** Popular donors sell out. If you hope to have more than one child with the same donor, ask about buying extra vials and storing them.

## Who uses donor sperm

Donor sperm is used by:

- Single women and people building a family on their own
- Lesbian and queer couples, including reciprocal IVF, where one partner's eggs are used and the other partner carries
- Heterosexual couples facing male factor infertility, or where there is a genetic condition they do not want to pass on
- Some surrogacy journeys, including families who need both donor eggs and donor sperm, or who choose a donated embryo instead

If you are planning a surrogacy journey with donor sperm, [this guide to surrogacy with donor sperm or donor embryos](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-with-donor-sperm-or-donor-embryos-canada) goes deeper.

## IUI or IVF?

Donor sperm is used either in **IUI** (washed sperm placed in the uterus around ovulation, less invasive and less expensive per attempt) or in **IVF** (eggs fertilized in the lab, often used when there are other fertility factors or a surrogate or egg donor is involved). Which makes sense depends on your age, history and goals, so that is a conversation for your fertility doctor. Our [step-by-step guide to IVF in Canada](/knowledge-centre/ivf-step-by-step-canada) explains a cycle.

## What it costs

I am going to be honest here: costs for donor sperm vary widely, and I am not going to quote you another company's prices. What you will pay depends on:

- The bank, and whether the donor is ID release (willing to be contacted later) or not
- How many vials you buy, and the type of vial (some are prepared for IUI, some for IVF)
- Shipping and import handling, which can be significant because frozen sperm travels in special tanks
- Your clinic's fees for receiving, storing and thawing the sperm
- Storage fees if you keep extra vials for siblings
- The treatment itself, IUI or IVF, and medications

Some provinces help fund certain fertility treatments, and the rules are different everywhere. See [which provinces help pay for IVF](/knowledge-centre/faqs/which-provinces-fund-ivf-in-canada). Some of your out-of-pocket costs may also qualify for the medical expense tax credit, which we cover in [the medical expense tax credit for surrogacy and IVF](/knowledge-centre/medical-expense-tax-credit-surrogacy-ivf).

My advice: before you choose a donor, get your clinic's fees and the bank's fees (including shipping) in writing and add them up together.
