If you are here, you have probably cried in a bathroom at a baby shower. You have probably smiled through someone else's pregnancy announcement and then gone home and fallen apart. You may have stopped answering texts, or started dreading family dinners because someone always asks "so when are you two having kids?"

I want you to know: what you are feeling has a name. It is grief. And it deserves the same care as any other kind of grief.

I grew up in this field, and I have sat with so many people in exactly the place you are now. I know how lonely it can feel. So let me talk to you like a big sister would.

*This article is general information, not mental health or medical advice. If you are struggling, please reach out to a professional or one of the supports listed below.*

## Infertility grief is real grief

Infertility grief is often invisible. There is no funeral, no casseroles, no time off work. Sometimes there is not even a clear moment of loss, just a slow series of "not this month" that adds up over years.

You might be grieving:

- Pregnancies that ended, at any stage
- Pregnancies that never happened
- The idea of carrying your own baby
- A genetic connection, if you are moving to donor eggs, sperm or embryos
- The timeline you thought your life would follow
- The version of yourself who was not afraid of a pregnancy test

All of those count. You do not have to rank your grief against anyone else's.

I always say: to grieve is to know love. You are grieving this hard because you already love the family you are hoping for.

## What is normal

Many people going through infertility notice some of these:

- Sadness that comes in waves, often around cycle days, test days and anniversaries
- Anger, including at friends who got pregnant easily, which can then bring guilt
- Anxiety, especially around appointments, results and money
- Feeling out of control, or like your body has betrayed you
- Pulling away from people, social media or events with children
- Trouble sleeping, concentrating or enjoying things you used to love

None of this means you are weak or doing it wrong. It means you are carrying something heavy.

## When to get more help

Please reach out to your doctor or a mental health professional if:

- The sadness or worry does not lift, or gets in the way of work, sleep or daily life for weeks at a time
- You are using alcohol or other things to cope more than you would like
- You feel hopeless, or you are having thoughts of harming yourself

**If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 9-8-8 anytime in Canada.** If you are in immediate danger, call 9-1-1.

## How infertility affects couples

Infertility puts real strain on relationships, even strong ones. Some common patterns:

- **Grieving differently.** One partner wants to talk about it all the time, the other copes by staying busy. Neither is wrong. They are just different.
- **Uneven load.** In many couples, one person carries most of the medical side: the needles, the appointments, the procedures. The other may feel helpless or left out.
- **Decision fatigue.** One more cycle? Donor eggs? Surrogacy? How much money? These are huge decisions, often made under pressure.
- **Intimacy turning into a schedule.** When sex has been tied to timing and tests, it can stop feeling like connection.

Some things that help:

- **Set a time to talk about fertility, and time when you do not.** Some couples agree on a weekly check in and protect the rest of the week.
- **Say what you need.** "I need you to just listen" and "I need you to help me decide" are very different requests.
- **Make decisions together, with a plan.** Agree ahead of time how many cycles you will try, or what budget feels okay, so you are not deciding in the middle of heartbreak.
- **Get outside help early.** A counsellor is not a sign your relationship is failing. It is a tool to keep it strong.

If you are single and doing this alone, you carry all of the above without a built-in partner to lean on. Please build a circle: a friend who comes to appointments, an online community of others going solo, a counsellor who gets it.

## Support options in Canada

**Fertility counsellors.** Look for a counsellor, psychologist or social worker with experience in fertility, pregnancy loss or third party reproduction. Many fertility clinics have a counsellor on staff or a referral list. Ask yours. Counselling is also often part of screening for donor and surrogacy journeys, and many people find it useful to keep going afterward.

**Your workplace or school.** Many employers and post-secondary schools offer Employee Assistance Programs (EAP) or student counselling with a few free sessions. Workplace health benefits may also cover some therapy costs. Check your plan.

**Your family doctor.** Your doctor can help with sleep, mood and anxiety, and can refer you to mental health services in your area.

**Support groups.** Talking to people who truly understand can be a huge relief. Fertility Matters Canada is a national, non-profit organization that offers education and support for Canadians facing infertility, and it is a good place to look for peer support and resources. Some clinics and hospitals also run groups, including groups for pregnancy loss.

**Online communities.** There are online groups for nearly every situation: MRKH, recurrent loss, two dad families, single parents by choice, donor egg families. Find ones that feel kind and well moderated, and step away from any that leave you feeling worse.

**Crisis support.** 9-8-8 is Canada's suicide crisis helpline, available by phone and text, 24 hours a day.

## Moving to donors or surrogacy has its own grief

When families move to donor eggs, donor sperm or a surrogate, there is often a mix of hope and loss. You can be thrilled to have a path forward and still mourn the path you hoped for. That is okay. You do not need to have "finished" grieving before you start.

Our article on [coming to terms with using an egg donor](/knowledge-centre/coming-to-terms-with-using-an-egg-donor) talks about this in more depth. For surrogacy, many intended mothers find that connection with their baby grows through the pregnancy in ways that surprise them.

## Small things that help

- Give yourself permission to skip the baby shower
- Mute or unfollow accounts that hurt right now
- Have a short answer ready for nosy questions ("We are not sharing family plans right now")
- Plan something good for the day after a big appointment
- Move your body gently, get outside, and try to sleep
- Write things down. Many people find journaling helps sort out what they are feeling
