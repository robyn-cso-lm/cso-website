Every week I hear from families outside Canada who are trying to figure out where they can legally build their family. Some live in countries where surrogacy is banned. Some live where it is allowed but only for certain people. Some are just trying to understand why the rules are so different from one place to the next.

This article gives you the big picture. It is not a country by country legal guide, and it cannot be, because surrogacy laws change often, and sometimes quickly.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Surrogacy laws change. Before you make any plans, check the current law in your home country and in the country where your baby will be born, with a lawyer qualified in each.*

## Why there is no single "world" answer

Countries have taken very different approaches to surrogacy, based on their own values about family, money, women's bodies and children's rights. Broadly, most fall into one of three groups:

1. **Surrogacy is banned**, in all or nearly all forms
2. **Only altruistic surrogacy is allowed**, where the surrogate is not paid beyond her expenses
3. **Compensated surrogacy is allowed**, where the surrogate receives a fee

Within each group there are big differences: who can use surrogacy, how parentage is transferred, whether agencies are allowed, and whether citizens can go abroad.

## Countries where surrogacy is banned

Several European countries do not allow surrogacy agreements at all. Well known examples include **France, Germany, Italy and Spain**. In these countries, surrogacy contracts are generally not recognized, and arranging surrogacy can carry penalties.

Italy went a step further in 2024, passing a law that makes surrogacy a crime for Italian citizens even when it is done in another country.

Families from countries with bans sometimes pursue surrogacy abroad. That can create serious problems when bringing a baby home, including delays with citizenship, travel documents and recognition of the parents. If you live somewhere surrogacy is banned, please get legal advice in your home country *before* you start.

## Countries that allow only altruistic surrogacy

In these countries, a surrogate can be reimbursed for reasonable expenses but cannot be paid a fee. Examples include:

- **Canada.** Under the federal *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*, paying a surrogate is a crime. Surrogates are reimbursed for receipted, pregnancy-related expenses. Parentage is handled by each province. Read [the legal framework for surrogacy in Canada](/knowledge-centre/legal-framework-canada) and [the AHRA explained](/knowledge-centre/assisted-human-reproduction-act-explained).
- **The United Kingdom.** Surrogacy is altruistic. The surrogate is treated as the legal mother at birth, and intended parents apply to court for a parental order afterward. The UK has been reviewing its surrogacy law for several years.
- **Australia.** Altruistic surrogacy is allowed under state and territory laws, which differ from each other. Commercial surrogacy is not allowed, and some states also make it an offence for their residents to arrange commercial surrogacy overseas.
- **New Zealand.** Altruistic surrogacy is allowed with approval processes. Commercial surrogacy is not.
- **India.** India was once a major destination for international surrogacy. Commercial surrogacy is now banned, and altruistic surrogacy is limited to eligible Indian residents who meet specific conditions.

## Countries and states that allow compensated surrogacy

The best known example is the **United States**, where surrogacy law is set state by state. Many states allow compensated surrogacy with clear legal processes, sometimes including parentage orders before birth. A few states restrict it. Laws continue to change: New York, for example, legalized compensated gestational surrogacy in 2021.

Some other countries, including a few in Eastern Europe, Central Asia and Latin America, have allowed compensated surrogacy at various times. Rules in some of these places have changed several times in recent years, especially for foreign intended parents, and some have been affected by conflict and political change. I am deliberately not listing them here, because the situation shifts too often to be reliable in an article like this.

## Countries that closed the door to foreigners

A pattern you will see over and over: a country becomes popular for international surrogacy, problems or scandals follow, and the government restricts it. **Thailand** restricted surrogacy for foreigners in 2015. India's changes followed a similar path.

This matters for you because the rules can change partway through a journey. Choose a country with a stable legal framework, and ask your lawyer what happens if the law changes after you start.

## What international families should check

Wherever you live, here are the questions to answer before you begin:

- **Is surrogacy legal where the baby will be born,** and is it open to people from my country, my relationship status and my family type?
- **How will I become the legal parent** where the baby is born?
- **Will my home country recognize me as the parent** when I get home?
- **How will my baby get citizenship and a passport** to travel home?
- **Is there anything in my home country's law** that makes surrogacy abroad an offence?
- **Who are my lawyers,** both where the baby is born and at home?

## Where Canada fits

Canada is one of the few countries where surrogacy is legal, regulated, and open to international intended parents, including same-sex couples and single people. Babies born in Canada are Canadian citizens. Because surrogacy here is altruistic, matching can take time, and families need to be prepared for that.

If you are an international family thinking about Canada, read [surrogacy in Canada for international intended parents](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-for-international-intended-parents-canada) and [can intended parents from outside Canada do surrogacy in Canada?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-international-intended-parents-do-surrogacy-in-canada) For a direct comparison with the US, see [surrogacy in Canada vs the United States](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-canada-vs-united-states).
