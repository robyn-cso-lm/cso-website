If you are thinking about becoming a surrogate, you have probably already thought about the pregnancy itself. But somewhere along the way, you, your partner or your mom has probably asked the harder question: what if something goes wrong? Who looks after my family?

That is a smart question, and you deserve a clear answer. I have been around surrogacy since I was 4 years old, and protecting surrogates has always been at the centre of how we work. Here is how insurance usually works for surrogates in Canada.

*This article is general information, not legal, insurance or medical advice. Your independent lawyer will review your surrogacy agreement with you, and a licensed insurance advisor can answer policy questions.*

## The three kinds of protection

When we talk about insurance for surrogates, we usually mean three different things:

1. **Life insurance**, so your family is financially protected if you die during the journey.
2. **Disability or income protection**, in case pregnancy complications keep you off work.
3. **Health coverage**, meaning your provincial health card and any extended benefits you already have.

Let's go through each.

## Life insurance: purchased by the intended parents

In Canadian surrogacy, it is standard practice for the intended parents to buy a life insurance policy on the surrogate for the length of the journey, usually starting before the embryo transfer and continuing for a period after the birth. The beneficiary is the surrogate's chosen person, usually her partner or a trust for her children. It is not the intended parents.

Some things to know:

- **The amount is set in your agreement.** Coverage amounts vary, and your lawyer can tell you whether the amount proposed seems reasonable for your family.
- **You will likely go through underwriting.** That means a health questionnaire, and sometimes a nurse visit or blood work. This is normal.
- **It costs the intended parents, not you.** The premium is a journey expense they cover.
- **Timing matters.** Ask for written confirmation that the policy is in force before your transfer. A good agency will track this for you.
- **It is separate from your own insurance.** If you already have life insurance through work or privately, keep it. Check whether your existing policy has any exclusions related to pregnancy or surrogacy, and ask your advisor if you are unsure.

This is not a payment to you, and it does not conflict with the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*. It is protection for your family, and it is one of the expenses the agreement asks intended parents to carry. You can read more about the law in [the AHRA explained](/knowledge-centre/assisted-human-reproduction-act-explained).

## Disability and lost income

Pregnancy can surprise anyone. Bed rest, a complication or a difficult recovery can mean time off work you did not plan for.

Here is how that is usually handled in Canada:

- **Lost wages can be reimbursed, with medical sign-off.** Under the federal reimbursement regulations, a surrogate can be reimbursed for lost work income during pregnancy only when a qualified medical practitioner certifies in writing that continuing to work could pose a risk to her health, the pregnancy or the baby. Your agreement will explain how this works.
- **EI maternity benefits.** If you qualify, surrogates can receive Employment Insurance maternity benefits after the birth or around it. Surrogates are not eligible for EI parental benefits, since those are for the people caring for the baby. Our article on [EI and parental leave](/knowledge-centre/ei-parental-leave-for-intended-parents) explains the split, and [will surrogacy affect my job or maternity leave?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/will-surrogacy-affect-my-job-or-maternity-leave) has the short version.
- **Workplace short-term disability.** Some employer plans cover pregnancy-related disability. Read your plan or ask HR. Some plans treat surrogacy differently, so ask specifically.
- **Separate disability policies.** Some intended parents and agreements address extra disability coverage. Whether that is included is something to discuss with your lawyer before you sign.

The goal is simple: you should never be left financially worse off because you chose to help a family.

## Your provincial health card

Your prenatal care, delivery and most of your postpartum care are covered by your provincial health plan, just like any other pregnancy. The fertility clinic side (medications, monitoring and the transfer) is usually paid by the intended parents.

A few practical notes:

- **Keep your health card valid and up to date.** Check the expiry date now.
- **Moving provinces mid-journey?** Tell your case manager early. Coverage in a new province often has a waiting period, and you will want a plan.
- **Care in another province.** Your province generally covers medically necessary hospital and physician care elsewhere in Canada, but there can be exceptions and billing quirks. If your clinic or delivery hospital is in another province, ask about this in advance.
- **No health card right now?** That is not automatically a deal-breaker for screening, but it is something we need to plan around. See [do I need a health card to be a surrogate?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/do-i-need-a-health-card-to-be-a-surrogate)

## Extended health benefits

If you have extended health coverage through your work or your partner's, it may cover some prescriptions, counselling or paramedical services. Your agreement may say whether you are expected to use your own plan first, with the intended parents covering whatever is left. Ask questions if this is unclear. Also check whether your plan has a lifetime or annual maximum you might want to keep for your own family.

Medications for the transfer itself are normally paid by the intended parents and are among the reimbursable expenses listed in the regulations.

## Travel insurance

If you travel for the transfer or monitoring, or if your intended parents live elsewhere and you visit, ask whether travel medical coverage is needed and who pays for it. Most intended parents add it as a journey expense when it applies.

## Questions to ask before you sign

Bring this list to your independent lawyer:

- Who is buying my life insurance, how much is it, and when will it be in force?
- Who is my beneficiary, and can I choose?
- What happens if I need to stop working early? How are lost wages handled?
- If I have complications after the birth, how long do reimbursements continue?
- Is there any coverage for loss of reproductive organs or other serious complications?
- What happens if I move provinces?
- If the intended parents miss a premium, how will I know?

Your lawyer works for you, not the intended parents. Use them. Learn more in [do we need a lawyer for surrogacy?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/do-we-need-a-lawyer-for-surrogacy)

## The honest bottom line

Most surrogacy journeys go smoothly, and most surrogates never use any of this coverage. That is exactly why it is so easy to skip in the excitement of matching. Please do not skip it. Your family matters too.
