Maybe you found out in your teens, when your period never came and a doctor finally ordered an ultrasound. Maybe you have known for years and are only now ready to think about what it means for your family. However you got here, if you have MRKH, I want you to hear this first: being born without a uterus does not mean you cannot have a baby who is genetically yours.

For many women with MRKH, the path to parenthood is IVF using their own eggs, with a gestational surrogate carrying the pregnancy. I have walked alongside a lot of families on this exact road. Let me explain it honestly.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your fertility clinic will confirm what is right for your body.*

## What MRKH is, in plain words

MRKH stands for Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser syndrome. It is a condition you are born with. In MRKH, the uterus and the upper part of the vagina do not develop fully, or do not develop at all.

Here is the part that matters most for building a family: **in most women with MRKH, the ovaries are present and work normally.** That means your body usually makes eggs and the usual hormones. You go through puberty, you may notice cycle-like changes, but there is no period because there is no uterus to shed a lining.

MRKH is often described as affecting roughly 1 in every 4,500 to 5,000 people born female. Some women with MRKH also have differences in their kidneys or skeleton, so your specialist may have checked for those too.

## Can I use my own eggs?

In most cases, yes. Because your ovaries usually work, a fertility clinic can stimulate them with medication, retrieve eggs, fertilize them with your partner's sperm or donor sperm, and create embryos. Those embryos are then transferred to a gestational surrogate.

A few things that are a little different with MRKH:

- **Monitoring and retrieval may need some adjusting.** In some women with MRKH, the ovaries sit a little higher in the pelvis than usual. Your clinic will look at this on ultrasound and plan the retrieval in the way that works for your anatomy.
- **Cycle tracking works differently.** Since there is no period to mark the start of a cycle, your clinic uses bloodwork and ultrasound to time things.
- **Egg quality still follows age.** MRKH does not usually affect egg quality, but age does. If you are not ready to start a surrogacy journey yet, it is worth asking a fertility doctor whether freezing eggs or embryos now makes sense for you.

If you want a walkthrough of what an IVF cycle looks like, see [IVF step by step in Canada](/knowledge-centre/ivf-step-by-step-canada).

## Why a gestational surrogate

In gestational surrogacy, the surrogate has no genetic connection to the baby. The embryo is made from your egg and your partner's or a donor's sperm. The surrogate carries the pregnancy and gives birth, and you are the parents.

This is the only type of surrogacy we support at CSO. If you are curious about the difference, read [gestational vs traditional surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/faqs/gestational-vs-traditional-surrogacy) and [does the surrogate use her own egg?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/does-the-surrogate-use-her-own-egg)

In Canada, surrogacy is altruistic. Under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*, a surrogate cannot be paid. She can be reimbursed for receipted, pregnancy-related expenses, which the intended parents cover. Provincial law then decides how you become the legal parents after the birth.

## What about uterus transplants?

You may have read about uterus transplantation. It is real, and a small number of babies have been born this way around the world. It is still a highly specialized, limited option that involves major surgery and anti-rejection medication. If it interests you, ask your specialist whether any programs are available to you and what they involve. For most Canadian families with MRKH today, gestational surrogacy is the more established and accessible path.

## The emotional side nobody prepares you for

I want to spend some real time here, because the medical part is often the easier part.

Many women with MRKH were diagnosed young, sometimes as teenagers, often in a rushed appointment with very little support. Some were told "you can't have children" and nothing else. If that was you, it makes sense if you carried that sentence for a long time.

Here are some feelings I hear often:

- **Grief for the pregnancy you will not have.** You can be excited about your baby and still grieve not carrying them. Both are true at once.
- **Feeling different or "less than."** MRKH can touch your sense of being a woman, your body image and your relationships. None of that makes you less of anything.
- **Worry about the surrogate relationship.** Will I feel connected to my baby? Will I feel jealous of her? These are normal questions, and most intended mothers find that the connection is there, often from the very first ultrasound.
- **Fear of telling people.** You decide who knows about your diagnosis. Surrogacy does not require you to share your medical history with anyone outside your care team.

There is a strong, warm MRKH community online, and many women say that meeting others with the same diagnosis was the first time they felt truly understood. A counsellor with fertility experience can also help, either before or during your journey. Our article on [infertility grief and mental health](/knowledge-centre/infertility-grief-and-mental-health) has more on finding support in Canada.

## Things you can do while you prepare

- **Feel connected during the pregnancy.** Many intended parents attend ultrasounds, record their voices for the surrogate to play to the baby, and share updates. See [how involved intended parents can be](/knowledge-centre/faqs/how-involved-can-intended-parents-be-in-the-pregnancy).
- **Ask about inducing lactation.** Some intended mothers without a uterus are able to produce some breast milk with medication and pumping. It is not for everyone, and it takes planning. Read [inducing lactation for intended mothers](/knowledge-centre/inducing-lactation-intended-mothers).
- **Plan the money.** A typical Canadian surrogacy journey costs about $80K to $120K CAD all in, including IVF, legal and surrogate expenses. See our [surrogacy cost breakdown](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-cost-canada-2026).

## Questions to bring to your fertility doctor

- Are my ovaries in a typical position, and how will that affect monitoring and retrieval?
- How many retrievals do you expect I may need to build up enough embryos?
- Should I consider freezing eggs or embryos now, even before I have a surrogate?
- Do you recommend genetic testing of embryos in my case?
- Is there anything about MRKH that affects my overall health that I should check first?
