Maybe she brought it up over dinner. Maybe she has been talking about it for years and has finally filled out the form. However it happened, the person you love wants to carry a baby for another family, and you are trying to figure out what that means for you.

First, thank you for reading this. The fact that you are here tells me you care about doing this well.

I grew up in this field. My mother, Joanne Wright, founded Canadian Surrogacy Options in 1992, and I have been around surrogacy since I was 4 years old. I have watched a lot of partners walk this road. The journeys that feel the best almost always have one thing in common: a partner who understood what they were signing up for, and who felt included rather than pulled along.

So let me walk you through it honestly.

## You are part of this journey too

Surrogacy is not something your partner does alone while you watch from the sidelines. In Canada, partners are part of the process in real, practical ways:

- **Screening:** Most fertility clinics and agencies want to meet the surrogate's partner. You may be asked to do a psychological screening or counselling session, and some clinics ask partners for basic health screening as well. Your clinic will tell you exactly what they need from you.
- **The surrogacy agreement:** Partners are commonly asked to be part of, or sign onto, the surrogacy agreement. That is why you will want to read it carefully and ask questions. You can learn more in [What is a surrogacy agreement?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-is-a-surrogacy-agreement)
- **Legal advice:** Your partner will have her own independent lawyer, separate from the intended parents' lawyer. If you are part of the agreement, expect to be involved in that conversation too. See [Do we need a lawyer for surrogacy?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/do-we-need-a-lawyer-for-surrogacy)

*This article is general information, not legal or medical advice. Your partner's lawyer and fertility clinic are the right people for questions about your own situation.*

## The honest version of what the months ahead look like

Here is what I want every partner to know before the first appointment.

**There will be appointments. Lots of them.** Screening, monitoring, transfer day, early ultrasounds and then regular prenatal care. Some of these may be in another city. You do not have to attend every one, but it helps to talk early about which ones matter to her and how you will handle work, childcare and travel.

**There will be medications.** Before a transfer, surrogates usually take a medication protocol prescribed by the clinic, and some of it may be injections. Many partners end up being the one who helps with those. If needles make you queasy, say so now. There are other ways to help.

**There may be waiting and disappointment.** Not every transfer works the first time, and some journeys take longer than anyone hoped. The [surrogacy timeline guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-timeline-canada) gives you a realistic picture of how long the whole thing can take. Plan for patience.

**There will be a pregnancy in your home.** Morning sickness, tiredness, a growing belly, restrictions on heavy lifting later on. All the normal pregnancy things, with one big difference: the baby is going home with another family.

## What about our own kids?

If you have children, they will notice. In my experience, kids handle surrogacy beautifully when the adults explain it simply and honestly, at a level that fits their age. Something like "Mommy is helping another family grow their baby, because their tummy can't" goes a long way with little ones.

Agree together on how you will talk about it, so you are telling the same story. We have a whole article on [how to talk about surrogacy with your family](/knowledge-centre/how-to-address-surrogacy-with-your-family) that can help.

## Money: what is covered and what is not

In Canada, surrogacy is altruistic. Under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*, surrogates cannot be paid. What they can receive is reimbursement for receipted, pregnancy-related expenses, which are paid by the intended parents (often through an escrow or trust account).

What that means for your household:

- Your family should not end up out of pocket for things related to the pregnancy, as long as expenses are documented and fall within the agreement.
- Keep receipts. All of them. It is much easier to track as you go than to rebuild later.
- Talk about things like lost wages, childcare during appointments and travel before the agreement is signed, so there are no surprises.

You can read the details in our [surrogacy reimbursements guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-reimbursements-canada-guide) and [what expenses are reimbursed to a surrogate](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-expenses-are-reimbursed-to-a-surrogate).

## How to actually support her

Partners ask me this all the time. Here is what surrogates tell me helped the most:

- **Show up for the big moments.** Transfer day, the first ultrasound, the birth if she wants you there. Ask her which ones matter most.
- **Take something off her plate.** Meals, school runs, laundry, the late night pharmacy trip. Practical help is love.
- **Be her soft place to land.** Hormones are real. Waiting is hard. Some days she will want to talk about it and some days she will not.
- **Respect her relationship with the intended parents.** She may become close with them, texting regularly and sharing updates. That relationship is part of what makes the journey meaningful for her. You are allowed to be part of it too.
- **Protect her rest.** In the later months especially, sleep and downtime are not luxuries.

## Look after yourself, too

This part gets skipped, so I want to say it clearly: partners have feelings about surrogacy too. Pride, worry, impatience, occasionally a little resentment about the appointments or the attention. All of that is normal.

Talk to her about it. Talk to a friend. If your clinic or agency offers counselling, consider using it yourself. At CSO, our team supports the whole surrogate household, not just the person who is pregnant. You can read more in [what emotional support surrogates get](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-emotional-support-do-surrogates-get).

## After the baby is born

When the baby goes home with their parents, many surrogates feel a beautiful mix of pride, relief and a little emptiness. Her body is also recovering from a pregnancy and birth. This is a time to be extra gentle with her, keep helping around the house and watch for signs that she is having a harder time than expected.

Most surrogates say the moment they handed that baby to the parents was one of the proudest of their lives. Partners often say the same thing about watching her do it.

## Questions to talk through together before you say yes

- Why does she want to do this, and how do you feel about it?
- How will we handle appointments, travel and childcare?
- What kind of relationship do we hope to have with the intended parents?
- Are we comfortable with the medical side, including medications and the risks of pregnancy?
- How will we explain this to our kids, our parents and our friends?
- Are we both comfortable with the decisions written into the agreement?

If you can talk through those openly, you are already in a good place.
