If you are a parent through egg, sperm or embryo donation, you may lie awake wondering what your child will think about all this one day. If you are a donor, you may wonder whether a stranger with your eyes will reach out in twenty years. And if you are donor-conceived yourself, you may be reading this because a DNA test just told you something big.

Wherever you are sitting, this article is for you.

I have a personal stake in this. I was an egg donor five times, and I have run an egg donation agency since 2007. I have watched the world shift from "anonymous forever" to "anonymity is not really possible anymore," and I think that shift is mostly a good thing. Let me explain why, and what it means for you.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. A lawyer in your province can tell you how the law applies to your situation.*

## Why openness matters

For a long time, families were told it was kinder not to tell children they were donor-conceived. We know much better now. Many donor-conceived adults have spoken publicly about how hard it was to find out late, by accident, or from a DNA test, and how much it hurt to feel that something about their own story was kept from them.

The general guidance from counsellors who work in this field today is:

- **Tell your child early**, in simple age-appropriate words, so it is always part of their story rather than a shocking reveal.
- **Keep telling it** as they grow, adding detail as they can understand more.
- **Make it safe to ask questions**, and to have feelings about it, including curiosity about the donor.

Our article on [talking to your child about egg donation](/knowledge-centre/talking-to-your-child-about-egg-donation) has practical scripts, and they work just as well for sperm and embryo donation. [Coming to terms with using an egg donor](/knowledge-centre/coming-to-terms-with-using-an-egg-donor) is there for the parent side of those feelings.

## How consumer DNA tests changed everything

Consumer DNA tests, the kind you buy online and send a saliva sample to, have made true donor anonymity very hard to guarantee.

Here is how it works in real life:

- A donor-conceived person takes a test and is matched with genetic relatives in the company's database.
- Even if the donor never tested, a cousin, aunt or half sibling may have.
- With a few matches and some family tree research, people can often identify their donor.

That means:

- **For parents:** assume your child may one day find their donor or donor siblings, whether you told them or not. It is much better if they hear about their conception from you first.
- **For donors:** a promise of anonymity from a clinic or bank is not a promise that no one will ever find you. If you are donating today, go in expecting that contact is possible.
- **For donor-conceived people:** you are not doing anything wrong by testing or searching. Go gently, though. A donor or half sibling may not know about you, and first contact can be emotional for everyone.

## Donor siblings

A donor-conceived person may have genetic half siblings in other families who used the same donor. With embryo donation, they may even have full genetic siblings being raised in the donor family.

Ways people connect:

- **Bank or clinic sibling registries**, where families can choose to share contact details
- **Independent donor sibling registries** where families and donor-conceived people register by donor number
- **DNA test matches**
- **Families sharing donor numbers in online groups**, by choice

Many families love having a wider "donor family" and choose to connect while children are young. Others prefer to wait and let the child decide. Both are valid. What matters is that you make the choice deliberately and stay open to your child's wishes as they grow.

If you are still choosing a donor, ask about family limits and sibling registries. We cover this in [how to choose a sperm donor](/knowledge-centre/choosing-a-sperm-donor) and [how to choose an egg donor](/knowledge-centre/how-to-choose-an-egg-donor).

## What Canadian law does and does not guarantee

This is the part where I need to be very honest, because people often assume there is more protection than there is.

### What Canada does not have

- **No national donor registry.** Canada does not have a federal registry where donor-conceived people can look up their donor. An earlier federal agency that was meant to oversee assisted reproduction was dissolved after a 2010 Supreme Court of Canada decision on the division of federal and provincial powers.
- **No general legal right to know your donor's identity.** In most of Canada, donor-conceived people do not have a legal right to identifying information about their donor. A well-known British Columbia court case about this question ended, on appeal, without creating that right.
- **No national legal limit on donor offspring.** Limits on how many families can use one donor are generally set by banks and clinics, not by law.

### What does exist

- **Health Canada record keeping.** Under the federal Safety of Sperm and Ova Regulations, establishments that handle donor sperm and eggs must keep records so donations can be traced for safety reasons. Those records are for safety, not for helping children find donors.
- **ID release donors.** Many sperm banks, and many egg donation programs, offer donors who have agreed that a child can learn their identity later. This is a contract promise, not a guarantee from the government.
- **Your own agreements.** In known donation, embryo donation and many egg donations, the written agreement can set out what information is shared and whether contact is welcome. See [the ovum donation agreement and independent legal advice](/knowledge-centre/ovum-donation-agreement-canada).
- **Provincial changes.** Provinces control a lot of family law, and some have been looking at the question of a donor-conceived person's right to know their origins. Quebec's recent family law reforms touched on this. Rules may change, so check the current law in your province.

### Being a donor does not make you a parent

In general, under provincial parentage laws, a donor is not a legal parent just because they donated, when the child was conceived through assisted reproduction. Contact from a donor-conceived adult does not change that. There are exceptions where everyone agreed in writing, before conception, that a known donor would be a parent. See [using a known sperm donor](/knowledge-centre/known-sperm-donor-canada).

## For donors: getting ready for contact

If you donated, here is what I would want someone to tell me:

- Keep your contact information up to date with your bank, clinic or agency.
- Update your medical history if something important comes up in your family. It can matter to the child.
- Talk to your own partner and children about your donation, so it is not a shock if someone reaches out.
- If contact comes, you get to set boundaries. Kindness and honesty go a long way, even if you do not want an ongoing relationship.

## For donor-conceived people: you are allowed to want answers

Wanting to know where you come from is not ungrateful. It is human. Wanting to know your medical history is practical. If you are searching, find support: other donor-conceived people, a counsellor who understands donor conception, or both. And be gentle with your parents, too. Many of them were given advice decades ago that we now know was wrong.
