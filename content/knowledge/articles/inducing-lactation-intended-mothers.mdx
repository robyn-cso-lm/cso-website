If you are an intended mother, you may have spent a long time grieving the pregnancy you could not carry. For some women, breastfeeding is a piece of that dream they are not ready to let go of. Maybe it is the closeness. Maybe it is wanting to give your baby your own milk. Maybe it is simply wanting one part of the beginning to feel the way you always pictured it.

Here is something many people do not know: it is often possible to produce some breast milk without being pregnant. It is called **induced lactation**, and it may be an option for intended mothers, and for other non-gestational parents too.

I want to give you an honest overview so you can decide whether to explore it. I am not a doctor, and this is not something to try on your own. It is a conversation to start with your family doctor and a lactation consultant.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Induced lactation can involve prescription medications that carry real risks. Please talk to a qualified health care provider before you start anything.*

## What induced lactation is

Milk production is driven mainly by hormones and by regular stimulation of the breasts. In pregnancy, hormones prepare the breast tissue for months. After birth, frequent nursing or pumping tells the body to make milk.

Induced lactation tries to recreate parts of that process without a pregnancy. Depending on your health and your provider's advice, an approach might include:

- **Hormonal preparation** for a period of time, to help mimic some of the changes of pregnancy
- **Regular pumping**, usually with a hospital-grade double electric pump, often several times a day and sometimes overnight, for weeks before the baby arrives
- **Medications that may support milk supply**, which some doctors prescribe in certain situations
- **Herbal supplements**, which some people try, though evidence varies and "natural" does not always mean safe

There are published protocols that providers may use as a starting point, but a good provider will adapt any plan to your medical history.

## Medications: a real caution

Some medications used to boost milk supply are prescribed "off label," which means they are being used for something other than what they were originally approved for. Some have known side effects, and Health Canada has issued safety warnings about heart-related risks with at least one medication that is sometimes used this way. Hormonal medications are not safe for everyone either, especially people with certain cancers, blood clotting history or other conditions.

This is especially important for intended mothers whose path to surrogacy included cancer, a hysterectomy, or other medical conditions. If that is you, your oncologist or specialist needs to be part of the conversation. Our article on [surrogacy after a hysterectomy or cancer](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-after-hysterectomy-or-cancer) may also be helpful.

Please do not order medications online or take someone else's plan. Work with a provider who knows your history.

## Who might consider it

Induced lactation is something people explore in many situations:

- Intended mothers in a surrogacy journey
- Non-gestational mothers in two-mom families
- Adoptive parents
- Some trans and nonbinary parents, with guidance from a provider experienced in gender-affirming care

Being able to induce lactation does not depend on having been pregnant before, although people who have breastfed before may sometimes find it a little easier. Your provider can talk to you about your own body.

## Realistic expectations

I believe in radical honesty, so here it is: many people who induce lactation make **some** milk, but not always a full supply. Every amount counts. Even a little of your milk can be meaningful, and many families combine it with formula, donor milk or milk from their surrogate.

It also takes commitment. Weeks of pumping on a schedule, while also preparing for a baby, working and managing the emotions of a surrogacy journey, is a lot. Some parents find it deeply rewarding. Others start and decide it is not for them. Both outcomes are completely okay.

And if what you really want is the closeness, know that **skin-to-skin contact and bottle feeding with eye contact are powerful bonding tools too.** Your baby will know you, and love you, however they are fed.

## Practical tips if you want to explore it

- **Start early.** Many plans begin months before the due date, so raise this with your provider as soon as you have a confirmed pregnancy, or even before transfer.
- **Find a lactation consultant** (an IBCLC) who has experience with induced lactation. Ask your doctor or your local hospital for referrals.
- **Plan pumping around your life.** Think about work, travel to the birth and your sleep.
- **Learn about feeding tools,** like a nursing supplementer, a thin tube taped at the breast that lets baby get extra milk while nursing. Your lactation consultant can show you.
- **Tell your surrogate early** if you want to try nursing in the hospital, so it can be part of your birth plan. See [surrogacy birth plans and delivery day](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-birth-plan-and-delivery-day).
- **Check costs.** Some pump rentals, lactation consults and medications may be covered by workplace benefits. Keep your receipts, as some health expenses may count for tax purposes. Our article on the [medical expense tax credit](/knowledge-centre/medical-expense-tax-credit-surrogacy-ivf) explains more.

## Coordinating with your surrogate

If your surrogate also plans to pump, the two of you might share the job of feeding baby breast milk for a while. If she does not want to pump, that is her choice, and your efforts can stand on their own. Our article on [breast milk and surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/breast-milk-and-surrogacy) explains how surrogate pumping is arranged and reimbursed in Canada.

## Be gentle with yourself

Wanting to breastfeed after infertility or loss can carry a lot of emotion. If it works, wonderful. If it does not, or if you decide not to try, it is not one more thing your body "failed" at. Please be kind to yourself. Our article on [infertility grief and mental health](/knowledge-centre/infertility-grief-and-mental-health) talks about this kind of tender, lingering grief.
