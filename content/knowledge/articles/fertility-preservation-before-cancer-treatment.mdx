If you are reading this, you may have just heard the word "cancer," and now someone has added "fertility" to a list of things you need to think about this week. That is a lot. I am so sorry you are here.

I want to make this as simple as I can. You do not need to become an expert today. You just need to know your options exist, that time matters, and that you are allowed to ask for a fertility referral right now.

I have worked with many families whose path to parenthood went through cancer first. Some froze eggs, sperm, or embryos before treatment. Some did not get the chance and later built their families with donors or a surrogate. Every one of those paths led to real families.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your oncology team and a fertility specialist will decide together what is safe for you.*

## Why fertility may be affected

Some cancer treatments can affect fertility. Certain chemotherapy drugs, radiation near the pelvis or reproductive organs, some surgeries, and some hormone treatments can damage eggs, sperm, or the organs that make them. How likely this is depends on the type of treatment, the dose, your age, and other factors.

Not every treatment affects fertility, and some effects are temporary. Your oncologist can give you a sense of your own risk. If no one has raised it yet, it is completely okay for you to raise it.

## Why timing is urgent

Fertility preservation usually needs to happen **before** treatment begins, because treatment can harm eggs and sperm. That means decisions often have to be made quickly.

Ask your oncology team for a fertility referral as early as possible, ideally right after diagnosis. Many fertility clinics in Canada prioritize cancer patients and can see them within days. The fertility team will work with your oncologist on what is safe and how much time you have.

## Options for people with ovaries

**Egg freezing.** Hormone injections for roughly two weeks stimulate the ovaries, then eggs are retrieved and frozen. Many clinics can start stimulation at any point in the menstrual cycle, which can save time. For some hormone-sensitive cancers, doctors may adjust the medications used. Read [egg freezing in Canada](/knowledge-centre/egg-freezing-in-canada) for the general process.

**Embryo freezing.** If you have a partner or are using donor sperm, eggs can be fertilized and frozen as embryos. This involves the same stimulation and retrieval. It is worth knowing that frozen embryos belong to both people who created them, so their future use depends on both. Think carefully, and get advice, if the relationship is new or uncertain.

**Ovarian tissue freezing.** A piece of ovarian tissue is removed through surgery and frozen. Later, it may be transplanted back to restore some function. This option can be useful when there is no time for stimulation, or for children who have not gone through puberty. It is offered in some Canadian centres, so ask your team whether it is available to you.

**Ovarian protection during treatment.** Some patients receive medications intended to help protect the ovaries during chemotherapy, and some radiation plans use shielding or move the ovaries surgically. These are discussed case by case.

## Options for people with testes

**Sperm banking.** This is usually the quickest and simplest option. Semen samples are collected and frozen, ideally before any treatment starts. Several samples can be stored if time allows.

**Other options.** If sperm cannot be collected the usual way, a doctor may be able to retrieve sperm surgically. For children who have not gone through puberty, testicular tissue freezing may be offered in research or specialized settings.

## Options for children and teens

Fertility preservation for young people is a specialized area. Options depend on age and puberty stage. Paediatric oncology teams in Canada can connect families with fertility specialists who work with children. Parents, please ask. It is a conversation worth having even when it feels impossibly hard.

## Help with costs

This is one area where Canada does better than with elective treatment.

- **Some provinces fund fertility preservation for medical reasons.** Coverage, eligibility and what is included differ by province and can change, so ask the fertility clinic what is covered where you live.
- **Medications are often the biggest cost.** Some pharmaceutical companies and clinics have programs that reduce medication costs for cancer patients.
- **Charities can help.** There are Canadian charities that support young adults with cancer, including with fertility preservation costs. Ask your cancer centre's social worker or the fertility clinic what is available to you.
- **Workplace benefits** sometimes cover fertility medication.
- **Tax credits.** Some fertility costs may be eligible for the medical expense tax credit. See [medical expenses and tax credits for surrogacy and IVF](/knowledge-centre/medical-expense-tax-credit-surrogacy-ivf).

Storage fees are usually ongoing and may not be covered, so ask about those too.

## If you did not get the chance to preserve

Some people do not have time, or are not offered preservation, or the treatment worked out differently than expected. Please hear me: that does not mean the door to parenthood is closed.

Families build their lives after cancer through donor eggs, donor sperm, donated embryos, surrogacy, adoption, and combinations of those. Our articles on [egg donation after cancer or early menopause](/knowledge-centre/egg-donation-after-cancer-or-early-menopause) and [surrogacy after a hysterectomy or cancer](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-after-hysterectomy-or-cancer) are written for exactly this situation.

## Questions to ask your oncologist and fertility team

- How likely is my treatment to affect my fertility?
- How much time do I have before treatment must start?
- Which preservation options are safe for my type of cancer?
- What will my province cover, and what will I pay?
- After treatment, how long should I wait before trying to build a family?

## Look after your heart, too

Grieving the way you thought your family would happen is normal, even while you are fighting for your health. Please lean on counsellors, your care team, and the people who love you.
