If you are reading this after cancer treatment, or after being told your ovaries stopped working much earlier than expected, I want to start by saying: I am so sorry. You have already been through so much. The fact that you are still dreaming about a family says a lot about your strength.

I also want you to know that, for many people in your situation, there is still a path. Egg donation has helped so many families who thought the door had closed.

I grew up in this field and I founded Little Miracles in 2007. I have walked alongside many survivors and people with early menopause. Here is what I want you to know.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your oncology team and fertility clinic are the right people to talk to about your own health and whether pregnancy is safe for you.*

## Two different starting points, a similar path

**After cancer.** Some cancer treatments, like certain types of chemotherapy, radiation or surgery, can affect the ovaries. Some people were able to freeze eggs or embryos before treatment. Many were not, often because there simply was not time. If that is you, please do not carry guilt about it. You were fighting for your life.

**Early menopause or premature ovarian insufficiency (POI).** Sometimes the ovaries stop working well much earlier than expected, for reasons that are not always clear. It can come as a real shock, especially if you were not even thinking about trying yet.

In both situations, donor eggs can make it possible to create embryos using a donor's eggs and sperm from a partner or a donor.

## The first conversations to have

Before anything else, there are some important questions for your medical team.

**For survivors, talk to your oncologist:**
- Is it safe for me to try to get pregnant, and when?
- Are there any risks from my treatment history I should know about?
- Would carrying a pregnancy affect my risk of recurrence?

**For everyone, talk to a fertility clinic:**
- Is my uterus healthy enough to carry a pregnancy?
- Do I need any tests or treatment first?
- What would a donor egg cycle look like for me?

Sometimes the answer is that you can carry a pregnancy yourself using donor eggs. Sometimes the answer is that carrying is not safe or possible, and a gestational surrogate may be the best option. Some families need both a donor and a surrogate. If that might be you, [Do I Need an Egg Donor, a Surrogate, or Both?](/knowledge-centre/do-i-need-an-egg-donor-a-surrogate-or-both) and [Can you combine egg donation and surrogacy?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/can-you-combine-egg-donation-and-surrogacy) are good next reads.

## Making room for the grief

Survivors and people with POI often carry a double grief: the loss of the health they had, and the loss of the fertility they expected. That deserves space.

Some people tell me they feel they "should just be grateful to be alive." You can be grateful and sad at the same time. Both are true.

Fertility counselling is part of the donor egg process in Canada, and it can be a gentle place to sort through all of this. I also wrote [Coming to Terms With Using an Egg Donor](/knowledge-centre/coming-to-terms-with-using-an-egg-donor) for anyone feeling the weight of letting go of a genetic connection.

## Choosing a donor

When you are ready, choosing a donor is often the moment when hope starts to feel real again. Many families tell me reading a donor's personal statement was the first time in a long time they cried happy tears.

A few things that can help:

- Decide on your top three priorities before you look at profiles
- Bring any family or genetic questions to your clinic or a genetic counsellor
- Think about whether you want a donor who is open to future contact

[How to Choose an Egg Donor](/knowledge-centre/how-to-choose-an-egg-donor) walks you through reading a profile step by step.

You may also want to think about whether a fresh or frozen donor egg cycle makes more sense for your timeline. Your clinic can help you weigh that. [Fresh vs Frozen Donor Eggs](/knowledge-centre/fresh-vs-frozen-donor-eggs) explains the basics.

## Practical things to think about

**Timing.** For survivors, your medical team may recommend waiting a certain amount of time after treatment. Use that time to research, save and get your questions answered, so you are ready when you get the green light.

**Cost.** Egg donation is a real investment. Some provinces have fertility funding, though what is covered varies a lot. See [Which provinces fund IVF in Canada?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/which-provinces-fund-ivf-in-canada) and our full breakdown in [Egg Donation Costs in Canada](/knowledge-centre/egg-donation-costs-in-canada). If you want to share costs, [Shared-Cost Egg Donation](/knowledge-centre/shared-cost-egg-donation-canada) may help.

**Paperwork.** Your clinic may ask for medical records from your oncology team. Requesting them early can save weeks.

**Support.** Lean on your people. If friends and family do not quite understand, find others who do. Ask us about connecting with other families.

## Telling your child one day

Many families who used donor eggs after cancer or POI find it becomes part of a powerful family story: "We wanted you so much that a kind woman helped us bring you here." When you are ready to think about that, [Talking to Your Child About Egg Donation](/knowledge-centre/talking-to-your-child-about-egg-donation) is here for you.

## You are not starting from zero

I know it can feel like everything has been taken from you. But you are not starting from zero. You are starting from experience, resilience and a very clear sense of what matters. That is a powerful place to build a family from.
