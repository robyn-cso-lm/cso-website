Delivery day is the moment everyone has been working toward. For intended parents, it is the day they meet their child. For the surrogate, it is the day she gives birth and completes something extraordinary. Both of those things are true at the same time, in the same room, and that is exactly why a good birth plan matters so much.

I grew up in this field, and I have been around surrogacy births since I was 4 years old. The births that feel the most peaceful are almost never the ones where everything went "to plan." They are the ones where everyone talked about the plan early, honestly and kindly.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. The surrogate's OB or midwife and the hospital are the right people for questions about labour, delivery and medical care.*

## Start the conversation early

I suggest surrogates and intended parents start talking about the birth sometime in the second trimester, and come back to it in the third. You do not need every detail nailed down early. You do need to know where each other stands.

Remember the most important principle: **the surrogate is the patient.** Her body, her labour and her medical care are her decisions, made with her OB or midwife. The intended parents are deeply important people in that room, but medical decisions about the birth belong to her and her care team. A good birth plan respects that and also makes room for the intended parents' hopes.

## What to talk about in your birth plan

Here are the questions I encourage every match to work through together.

**Where will the baby be born?**
Usually this is the hospital where the surrogate's OB or midwife has privileges, close to where she lives. If the intended parents are travelling from another province or country, they will want to know the hospital early so they can plan where to stay.

**Who will be in the room?**
Hospitals often limit the number of support people in a labour room, and those rules can change. Talk about who the surrogate wants with her (often her partner or a support person) and whether one or both intended parents will be there for the birth itself. Some surrogates love having the intended parents right there. Others prefer them to wait just outside until the baby arrives. Both are completely valid.

**What happens in the first moments after birth?**
Who cuts the cord? Who holds the baby first? Does the surrogate want to see or hold the baby? Will skin-to-skin happen with an intended parent? These moments are tender, and talking about them ahead of time prevents a lot of awkwardness.

**What if plans change?**
Labour does not read birth plans. A planned vaginal birth may become a caesarean. A planned induction may happen on a different day. In an operating room, the number of support people is usually even more limited. Agree in advance what happens if the birth looks different from what you pictured.

**Feeding the baby.**
Will the baby be formula fed, or does the surrogate want to offer or pump breast milk? This is entirely her choice and should be talked about in advance, not decided in the moment.

**Photos and announcements.**
Who is taking photos? Is anyone posting on social media, and when? Does the surrogate want to share the news with her own friends and family, or wait until the parents have announced?

## Let the hospital know before you arrive

Some hospitals have experience with surrogacy births and some do not. It helps a lot to give the hospital a heads up before labour starts. Many of our families arrange a pre-registration or a conversation with the labour and delivery unit, and bring copies of key documents.

Your lawyers can tell you which documents the hospital may need to see, and how the birth registration works in your province. Our FAQ on [who is on the birth certificate after surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/faqs/who-is-on-the-birth-certificate-after-surrogacy) is a good place to start.

Things that can help on the day:

- A one-page summary of the birth plan for nurses, with names and roles of everyone involved
- Clear contact information for the intended parents and the surrogate's support person
- Copies of any documents the lawyers have advised the hospital will need

## After the baby is born

In many surrogacy births, the hospital arranges for the baby to stay with the intended parents, sometimes in their own room. Whether this is possible depends on the hospital, so ask ahead.

Meanwhile, the surrogate is recovering just like any other person who has given birth. She is a postpartum patient with her own needs: rest, monitoring, pain management and time with her family.

A few gentle reminders for intended parents:

- **Visit her.** Even a short visit to say thank you means the world. Many surrogates treasure the moment of seeing the family together for the first time.
- **Keep her updated.** She has carried this baby for months. A text or photo after you leave the hospital matters more than you know.
- **Give her space when she needs it.** Some surrogates want lots of contact in the first days. Others need quiet. Ask.

For surrogates, I want to say this: whatever you feel after the birth is okay. Joy, relief, tears, exhaustion, pride, a little emptiness. Often all of them in one afternoon. Our article on [postpartum recovery for surrogates](/knowledge-centre/postpartum-recovery-for-surrogates) covers the weeks that follow.

## International and out-of-province intended parents

If the intended parents live outside the province or outside Canada, delivery day takes more planning. Think about:

- Arriving well before the due date, in case baby comes early
- Where they will stay after discharge while paperwork is finalized
- Travel documents for the baby, which can take time

Our guide to [surrogacy for international intended parents](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-for-international-intended-parents-canada) walks through this in more detail.

## A simple birth plan checklist

- Hospital name and labour and delivery contact
- Surrogate's support person or people
- Intended parents' role during labour and the birth itself
- Plan B if a caesarean is needed
- First moments: cord, first hold, skin-to-skin
- Feeding plan
- Photos and social media
- Visiting plan after birth
- Documents the hospital may need, as advised by your lawyers
