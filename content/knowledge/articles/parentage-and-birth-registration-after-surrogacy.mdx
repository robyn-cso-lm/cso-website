You have spent months, maybe years, getting to this point. Your baby is coming. And somewhere in the back of your mind is a very practical worry: how do we actually become this baby's legal parents, and whose names go on the birth certificate?

It is one of the most common questions I get, and it is a good one. Let me walk you through how it works in Canada, and why the answer depends on where the baby is born.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Parentage law changes, and the details depend on your province and your situation. Your surrogacy lawyer will confirm exactly what applies to you.*

## The key thing to understand: parentage is provincial

The federal *Assisted Human Reproduction Act* decides what is legal in surrogacy, such as the ban on paying surrogates. You can read about that in [the AHRA explained](/knowledge-centre/assisted-human-reproduction-act-explained).

But the federal law does **not** decide who a child's parents are. That is family law, and family law belongs to the provinces. So the steps, timing and paperwork for becoming the legal parents depend on the province where the baby is born, and sometimes on where you live.

Broadly, provinces fall into two groups:

1. **Provinces with an administrative process.** If certain conditions are met, intended parents can be recognized as parents without going to court. Ontario and British Columbia are the best known examples.
2. **Provinces where a court order is needed.** Intended parents (with their lawyer) apply to a court for an order or declaration that they are the parents, and the birth registration is then updated.

## Ontario: the declaration process

Ontario changed its law in 2016 with the *All Families Are Equal Act*, which updated the *Children's Law Reform Act*. Since then, many Ontario surrogacy births are handled without a court order.

In general terms, the Ontario process works like this when the conditions in the law are met:

- The surrogate and the intended parents signed a written surrogacy agreement **before the child was conceived**.
- Each party had **independent legal advice** before signing.
- There are no more than four intended parents.
- After the baby is born, the surrogate gives her **written consent** to give up her parental status. Ontario law says she cannot give that consent until the baby is at least 7 days old.
- Once she consents, the intended parents become the legal parents, and the birth registration reflects that.

During those first days, the agreement and the law usually allow the intended parents to care for the baby and make decisions together with the surrogate. In real life, most babies go home with their parents from the hospital while the paperwork catches up.

If the conditions are not met, or if something unusual happens, an Ontario court can still make a declaration of parentage. Your lawyer will tell you if your situation needs that. There is more on Ontario in [surrogacy in Ontario](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-in-ontario).

## British Columbia: the pre-conception agreement approach

British Columbia's *Family Law Act* also lets intended parents become parents without a court order when the rules are followed. In general terms:

- The surrogacy agreement must be in writing and made **before conception**.
- After the birth, the surrogate gives her **written consent** to the intended parents being the parents.
- The intended parents take the child into their care.

When those steps are in place, the intended parents are the legal parents and the birth is registered with them named. If the requirements are not met, a BC court can still be asked to make a declaration. See [surrogacy in British Columbia](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-in-british-columbia).

## Alberta and other provinces: court orders

In many other provinces, including Alberta, intended parents typically need a court order or declaration of parentage after the birth, supported by the surrogate's consent. Your lawyer prepares the application, and the timeline varies with the court.

This is normal and routine for experienced surrogacy lawyers. It is more paperwork, not more risk, when everyone is on the same page.

## Quebec

Quebec has its own civil law system, and it reformed its surrogacy rules in 2023. The process there is distinct from the rest of Canada and has specific requirements around the agreement and when it is signed. Please read [surrogacy for Quebec families](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-for-quebec-families) and work with a Quebec lawyer.

## What if the surrogate lives in a different province?

This is common. The baby is usually born in the surrogate's province, so that province's law generally governs the birth registration and parentage steps. If you live in Manitoba and your surrogate lives in Ontario, for example, you will likely be following Ontario's process.

This is one of the reasons your lawyer and your surrogate's lawyer need to be experienced in surrogacy, and why it helps to know early where your surrogate plans to deliver.

## International intended parents

If you live outside Canada, there are two separate questions:

1. Becoming the legal parents in Canada (handled as above).
2. Getting your baby recognized by your home country, and getting the right citizenship and travel documents.

The second part can take longer than the first. Start talking to a lawyer who understands your home country's rules well before the birth. Our guide for [international intended parents](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-for-international-intended-parents-canada) covers this.

## Practical things to do before the birth

- **Ask your lawyer for a parentage roadmap.** What happens after the birth, who signs what, and when.
- **Talk to the hospital ahead of time.** Our [birth plan and delivery day guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-birth-plan-and-delivery-day) explains why a hospital letter and a clear birth plan help.
- **Know who registers the birth.** In some provinces, the parents complete the birth registration; your lawyer will tell you who does what.
- **Plan for health coverage.** Ask your lawyer and the hospital how your baby is added to provincial health coverage while parentage is being finalized.
- **Update your wills and name a guardian.** See [wills, guardianship and insurance for intended parents](/knowledge-centre/wills-guardianship-and-insurance-for-intended-parents).
