Beta day. If you are an intended parent, you have probably been refreshing your phone since dawn. If you are a surrogate, you may have already peed on six sticks and you are wondering whether to say anything.

I have been on the receiving end of a lot of beta day phone calls, the joyful ones and the hard ones. This article is here to help you understand what the number actually means, so that whatever result comes in, you know what you are looking at.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Labs and clinics use different ranges and follow-up plans, and only your clinic can interpret your results.*

## What "beta" means

hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) is a hormone made by cells that will become the placenta, starting soon after an embryo implants. A "beta" is a blood test that measures the amount of hCG in the blood. The name comes from the part of the hormone the test detects.

Unlike a home pregnancy test, which just says yes or no, a beta gives you a number. That number is reported in units called mIU/mL (or IU/L, which is the same value).

## When the test happens

Most clinics book the first beta somewhere around 9 to 14 days after a blastocyst transfer. The exact day depends on the clinic and the embryo's age at transfer.

A word about home tests: many surrogates and intended parents test early at home. That is a personal choice. Just know that home tests can be negative too early, and a positive home test can also be affected by a trigger shot if one was used recently. The blood test is the one your clinic relies on.

## The first number

People always ask me, "What is a good number?" The honest answer is that there is a wide range of normal.

In general:

- Labs usually consider a very low result negative. Many use a threshold around 5 mIU/mL, but this varies by lab.
- A positive first beta can be anywhere across a broad range. Two healthy pregnancies can start with very different numbers.
- Twins can sometimes show higher numbers, but a high number does not confirm twins, and a single embryo transfer usually means a single pregnancy.

So one number on its own tells you only part of the story. That is why the second test matters so much.

## The second beta: watching the trend

If the first beta is positive, the clinic usually repeats it, often about two days later and sometimes again after that. What the clinic is watching is how the number changes.

In a healthy early pregnancy, hCG generally rises quickly. A widely used rule of thumb is that it roughly doubles every 48 to 72 hours in the early weeks. As the pregnancy goes on, the rise naturally slows down.

Some important nuances:

- **Doubling is a guideline, not a rule.** Many healthy pregnancies rise a little more slowly than the textbook doubling. Some pregnancies that rise well still end in loss.
- **Clinics look at the trend, not a single doubling time.** They will usually combine several numbers before drawing conclusions.
- **A falling number** usually means the pregnancy is not continuing. The clinic will guide next steps, including when to stop medications. Please do not stop any medication on your own.
- **A slowly rising or plateauing number** can mean a few different things, including a pregnancy that may not continue or, less commonly, a pregnancy in the wrong place (an ectopic pregnancy). Your clinic will monitor closely.

## Chemical pregnancy

Sometimes the first beta is positive, but the number then drops before a pregnancy can be seen on ultrasound. This is often called a chemical pregnancy or biochemical pregnancy.

It is a real loss. It is also, sadly, common in IVF and in natural conception. If this happens to you, you are allowed to grieve. To grieve is to know love. Our article on [when surrogacy does not go as planned](/knowledge-centre/when-surrogacy-does-not-go-as-planned) talks about how families and surrogates get through it together.

## What the numbers cannot tell you

Let me be very clear here, because Google will try to tell you otherwise:

- A beta cannot tell you whether the baby will be healthy.
- It cannot reliably tell you whether a pregnancy will continue past the early weeks.
- It cannot tell you the baby's sex.
- It cannot reliably tell you if there are twins.

What eventually gives you much more information is the first ultrasound, usually a few weeks after a positive beta, when the clinic looks for a gestational sac and then a heartbeat. Our guide [from positive test to OB care](/knowledge-centre/positive-pregnancy-test-to-ob-care-surrogacy) walks through those early weeks.

## Comparing numbers online

I know it is tempting. There are whole websites of other people's beta numbers. But comparing your number to a stranger's tells you very little, because every lab, every embryo, and every body is different. If you find yourself spiralling, close the tab and call your clinic nurse instead. That is what they are there for.

## Beta day in surrogacy

Beta day in a surrogacy journey has an extra layer: who hears the news first, and how. I always encourage surrogates and intended parents to decide this ahead of time. Some options:

- The clinic calls the surrogate, and she calls the intended parents.
- Everyone is on a group call together.
- The clinic calls the intended parents directly, with the surrogate's consent.

Talk about the plan for a positive result and for a negative one. It makes the day kinder for everybody. Our FAQ on [how involved intended parents can be during the pregnancy](/knowledge-centre/faqs/how-involved-can-intended-parents-be-in-the-pregnancy) touches on this kind of communication planning.

## Questions to ask your clinic on beta day

- What is my number, and what is your lab's cutoff for positive?
- When is my next beta?
- Should I continue all my medications exactly as before?
- When would the first ultrasound be?
- Who should I call if I have bleeding or pain?
