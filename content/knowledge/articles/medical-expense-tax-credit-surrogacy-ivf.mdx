When families look at the full cost of surrogacy or egg donation, one of the first questions is: "Can we get any of this back at tax time?"

The answer is yes, some of it, and it got better in 2022. Here is how Canada's medical expense tax credit (METC) works for fertility, surrogacy and donor costs, in plain language.

*I am not an accountant, and this is general information, not tax advice. Tax rules change, and the right claim depends on your income, your province and your situation. Please confirm everything with an accountant or tax professional before you file.*

## What the medical expense tax credit is

The METC is a non-refundable tax credit. That means it reduces the income tax you owe, but it does not create a refund on its own if you do not owe tax.

How it works, in general:

- You add up eligible medical expenses paid in any 12-month period ending in the tax year (and not claimed before).
- You subtract a threshold. For the 2025 tax year, the threshold was the lesser of 3% of your net income or a fixed dollar amount set by the CRA (that dollar amount is adjusted every year).
- The federal credit is then calculated on what is left over. Your province or territory has its own medical expense credit too.

Because of the threshold, couples often get a bigger benefit when the lower-income spouse or partner claims the family's expenses. Your accountant can tell you which way works best for you.

## IVF has been eligible for years

Before 2022, many core fertility costs could already be claimed, including:

- IVF procedures and related clinic services
- Prescribed fertility medications
- Certain lab and diagnostic tests
- Egg and sperm freezing and storage

Many families did not realize this, so if you did IVF in past years, ask your accountant whether you missed anything. The CRA generally allows you to adjust returns from earlier years within set time limits.

## What changed with the 2022 federal budget

Budget 2022 expanded the METC for families using surrogates and donors. The change has received Royal Assent and applies to the **2022 tax year and later**.

According to the CRA, intended parents can now claim:

1. **Reimbursements you pay to your surrogate or donor** for expenses that are eligible under the federal reimbursement regulations, and that would be eligible medical expenses. Examples include prescription medications and diagnostic testing.
2. **Fees paid to fertility clinics and donor banks in Canada** to obtain donor sperm or eggs, to help you become a parent.

The conditions, in general terms:

- The expense must be paid so that you (or you and your spouse or partner) can become a parent.
- It must be paid by you or your spouse or common-law partner.
- It must be **incurred in Canada**. Costs incurred outside the country do not qualify under this measure.
- For surrogate and donor reimbursements, the expense must fit the categories in the *Reimbursement Related to Assisted Human Reproduction Regulations*.

## What does not qualify

The CRA has been clear on a few things that cannot be claimed under this measure, including:

- Any payment to someone to be a surrogate or donor. That is illegal in Canada anyway under the *Assisted Human Reproduction Act*. See [the AHRA explained](/knowledge-centre/assisted-human-reproduction-act-explained).
- Maternity clothing, food, vitamins and general living expenses, even if they are reimbursed to your surrogate under your agreement.
- Hospital services received outside Canada.
- Anything you were or will be reimbursed for, for example by an insurance plan.

So not every reimbursement you pay your surrogate will be claimable. Your surrogacy reimbursements may include things like travel or maternity clothes that are allowed under the surrogacy rules but are not medical expenses for tax purposes.

## Agency fees and legal fees

Agency fees and legal fees are not medical expenses. As a general rule, families should not expect to claim them under the METC. If you think something unusual applies to you, ask your accountant.

## Egg donation and Little Miracles costs

If you are using an egg donor in Canada, part of your egg donation budget may be claimable. For example, at Little Miracles, the estimated total for an egg donation journey is about $18,950 to $22,700 CAD (higher for non-local donors), made up of the agency fee, donor expenses, the ovum agreement, the donor's legal advice, OHSS coverage and psychological evaluations. Some of the donor's eligible medical-type expenses, and some clinic fees for obtaining the eggs, may qualify. Others, like the agency fee and legal fees, generally will not.

Our [egg donation costs guide](/knowledge-centre/egg-donation-costs-in-canada) breaks down each piece, and your accountant can tell you which lines to claim.

## Keep these records

The CRA does not want receipts sent with your return, but you need to keep them in case they ask. For surrogate and donor reimbursements, the CRA has said you may need to show proof of reimbursement, including the surrogate's or donor's name and social insurance number.

Tips that save headaches:

- Keep a single folder (digital is fine) for every clinic invoice, pharmacy receipt and reimbursement record.
- If you pay through a trust or escrow account, ask for a yearly statement. See [what is escrow in surrogacy?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/what-is-escrow-in-surrogacy)
- Talk to your surrogate or donor early about the SIN requirement, so it is not a surprise at tax time. Handle her personal information with care.
- Track which expenses are medical and which are not as you go.

Our [surrogacy reimbursements guide](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-reimbursements-canada-guide) explains how receipts are organized during a journey.

## Provincial programs

Some provinces have their own fertility support, such as funded IVF cycles or provincial tax credits or rebates for fertility and surrogacy costs. These change, and if you receive a provincial grant or rebate, the CRA expects you to account for it when you claim the METC. See [which provinces fund IVF in Canada?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/which-provinces-fund-ivf-in-canada) and ask your accountant what your province offers.

## What about US surrogacy?

If you do your surrogacy in the United States, the 2022 measure generally will not help, because it requires expenses to be incurred in Canada. That is one of the many cost differences to weigh. Read [surrogacy in Canada vs the United States](/knowledge-centre/surrogacy-canada-vs-united-states).

## A real-world example, without numbers

Picture a couple who did IVF in Ontario, then matched with a surrogate. In the year of the transfer, they paid clinic fees, medications, and reimbursed their surrogate for her prescriptions and some diagnostic tests. Under the current rules, those items may be eligible. The surrogate's travel, maternity clothes and their own agency fee would generally not be. The couple's accountant decided which spouse should claim, and which 12-month period gave the best result.

Every family's version of this is different. That is exactly why an accountant is worth it.
