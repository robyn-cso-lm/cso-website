Maybe it started as a half joke over drinks. Maybe your best friend offered before you even asked. Maybe your partner's brother wants to help so your baby shares your family's genes. However it came up, someone you know and trust is willing to be your sperm donor, and now you are wondering how to actually do this the right way.

I love known donation when it is done well. I was an egg donor five times myself, and I have watched known donor relationships turn into something really special: an extra person in a child's life who loves them, with everyone clear on their role. I have also seen what happens when people skip the hard conversations and the paperwork. So let me give you the honest roadmap.

*This article is general information, not legal or medical advice. Your clinic and your lawyers will confirm what applies to you and your province.*

## What "known donor" means

A known donor (sometimes called a directed donor) is someone you choose personally, rather than an anonymous or ID release donor from a sperm bank. It is often:

- A close friend
- A relative of the partner who is not carrying, such as a brother or cousin, so the baby has a genetic link to both sides
- Someone you met through a community connection

Known donors are common for single women and for lesbian and queer couples, and they also show up in some surrogacy journeys.

## Screening and quarantine: the part people do not expect

When donor sperm is used at a Canadian fertility clinic, it falls under Health Canada's Safety of Sperm and Ova Regulations. Those rules apply to known donors too, not just bank donors. Being your friend does not exempt him from screening.

Typically, a known donor going through a clinic will have:

- A medical and social history questionnaire
- A physical exam
- Blood and urine tests for infectious diseases
- A semen analysis, to check his sperm is suitable for freezing and use
- Often, genetic carrier screening, which many clinics recommend or require

Then the sperm is usually frozen and quarantined, commonly for at least six months, before he is retested and the sperm can be released for use. That means a known donor journey through a clinic often has a built-in waiting period. Plan for it rather than being surprised by it.

There is a pathway called exceptional distribution, where a doctor may be able to use sperm that has not met every requirement (for example, without the full quarantine) if the recipient gives informed consent to the risks. Whether a clinic offers this, and in what situations, varies. Ask your clinic directly.

One more note: the federal rules treat sperm from a spouse or sexual partner differently from donor sperm. A friend who is helping you is a donor, not a partner, so the donor rules apply.

## What about at-home insemination?

Some people consider doing insemination at home with a known donor. I understand why: it is cheaper and feels more private. But please go in with your eyes open:

- **Health risk:** without clinic screening, testing and quarantine, there is a real risk of passing on an infection or a genetic condition no one knew about.
- **Legal risk:** in some provinces, how a child is conceived affects who is a legal parent. Home insemination can leave a donor's legal status less clear than clinic-assisted conception.
- **No paper trail:** clinics keep records. At home, you are relying on memory and goodwill.

If you are considering this, speak with a family lawyer in your province before you try, not after.

## Money: what the law allows

Under the Assisted Human Reproduction Act, it is a crime to pay a donor for his sperm, and that applies to friends and relatives just as much as strangers. Your known donor can be reimbursed for receipted expenses directly related to donating, such as travel to the clinic or parking, in line with the federal reimbursement regulations. No thank-you payments, no "gifts" that are really payment.

You will usually pay the clinic's fees for his screening, testing, freezing and storage. Ask your clinic for a written list of those fees early on.

## The donor agreement

A known donor agreement is a written contract between you (and your partner, if you have one) and the donor, and often the donor's partner. It is one of the most important pieces of this whole process.

A good agreement usually covers:

- **Intentions about parentage:** whether the donor will be a legal parent or not
- **Role in the child's life:** "uncle-like" figure, family friend, occasional contact, or something else
- **What the child will be told** and when
- **Use of the sperm:** for one child, siblings, or a maximum number of families
- **What happens to leftover sperm or embryos** if plans change, someone separates, or someone dies
- **Confidentiality:** who knows, and who tells whom
- **Expenses:** what will be reimbursed, with receipts
- **No financial responsibility** for the donor, if that is the intent

Even close friends see things differently once a real baby exists. Writing it down now protects the friendship as much as it protects the child.

## Independent legal advice (ILA)

Each side needs its own lawyer. You (the intended parents) have one lawyer, and the donor has a separate lawyer who gives him independent legal advice. This is the same principle we use for egg donors and surrogates, and you can read more in [the ovum donation agreement and independent legal advice, explained](/knowledge-centre/ovum-donation-agreement-canada).

ILA matters because it shows everyone understood what they were signing, and it gives the donor someone whose only job is to look out for him. Many clinics will ask for confirmation of a signed agreement and ILA before they proceed.

## Parentage: it depends on your province

In Canada, who is a legal parent is decided by provincial and territorial law, not federal law. This is where known donor journeys really need a lawyer in your province.

Some general patterns, which your lawyer will confirm:

- **Many provinces say a sperm donor is not a legal parent just because he donated**, when the child is conceived through assisted reproduction.
- **Some provinces allow more than two legal parents** in certain cases, if everyone agrees in writing before conception. Ontario and British Columbia are examples of provinces with rules that can recognize a known donor as a parent when that is what everyone intends.
- **How conception happens can matter.** Some laws treat clinic insemination differently from conception through sex, and the rules for home insemination can be less clear.
- **Moving provinces can complicate things.** If you live in one province and the baby is born in another, ask your lawyer about both.

For more on how parentage and birth registration work after assisted reproduction, see [parentage and birth registration after surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/parentage-and-birth-registration-after-surrogacy) and [the legal framework for surrogacy in Canada](/knowledge-centre/legal-framework-canada).

## The conversations to have before anything is signed

- How will we refer to the donor with our child? By name? As an uncle? As "the donor"?
- How often will he see the child, if at all? What if he moves away?
- What happens if he has children of his own later? Will the kids know they are genetic half siblings?
- What if he wants a bigger role later, or a smaller one?
- What if our relationship with him becomes strained?
- How do both sets of grandparents fit in?

I strongly recommend everyone, including the donor and his partner, speak with a fertility counsellor before moving forward. Many clinics require it for known donation.
