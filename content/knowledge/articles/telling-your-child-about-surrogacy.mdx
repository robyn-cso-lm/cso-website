You are holding your baby, or you are about to, and somewhere in the back of your mind is a question: how do I tell them how they came to be?

Here is the good news. You do not need one big, perfect conversation. The families I have seen do this well treat it as a story that grows with their child, told a little at a time, starting early, in words that fit their age.

I have been around surrogacy since I was 4 years old, so I have watched a lot of "surrogacy babies" grow into teenagers and adults. The ones who knew their story from the start almost always carry it with pride.

## Why telling early tends to go best

The general advice from counsellors who work with donor and surrogacy families is consistent: tell your child early, keep telling, and keep it honest. Long-running research following families created through surrogacy and donation has generally found that children do well, and that being told young, rather than finding out later, tends to go more smoothly for everyone.

There are a few practical reasons too:

- **There is no "reveal" to dread.** If your child has always known, there is no moment where their sense of self is turned upside down.
- **Secrets are hard to keep.** Grandparents, friends, old photos, medical forms and DNA tests all make it likely that a child will find out eventually. It is far better that they hear it from you.
- **It shows them there is nothing to hide.** The way you talk about it tells your child how to feel about it.

## Age by age

### Babies and toddlers (0 to 3)

Start telling the story now, even though they will not understand it. This is your practice time. Say it out loud while you rock them, give them a bath, or look at photos.

"We wanted you so much. Our bodies needed help, and a very kind woman named Sarah carried you in her tummy until you were ready to be born. Then you came home with us."

Use the surrogate's name if you have a relationship with her. It makes her a real person rather than a mystery.

### Preschoolers (3 to 5)

Little kids love stories about themselves. Keep it simple and warm, and repeat it often.

- "You grew in Sarah's tummy because Mommy's tummy could not hold a baby."
- "Daddy and Papa needed help from a surrogate to have you."
- "The doctor helped put the tiny start of you into Sarah's tummy."

At this age, children may ask the same question again and again, or tell strangers at the grocery store. That is completely normal. It means the story feels safe.

### School age (6 to 10)

Now they start understanding how babies are usually made, and they may have more detailed questions. You can add more facts:

- How doctors helped make the embryo
- Whose egg and sperm were used, including if a donor helped
- Why Sarah wanted to help, and that she did not keep them because she was always helping your family have *their* baby

They may also notice that their story is different from friends' stories. Reassure them that families are made in lots of ways, and all of them are real families.

### Tweens and teens (11 and up)

Teens can handle the full picture, including the medical and legal side, and they may have strong feelings. Some are curious, some are proud, and some are not that interested. Follow their lead.

This is also when questions about identity, genetics and meeting the surrogate or a donor may come up. Answer honestly, and if a donor was involved, see [donor-conceived people, their rights and DNA tests](/knowledge-centre/donor-conceived-people-rights-and-dna-tests).

## Language that helps

- **Use real words.** "Surrogate," "embryo," "egg donor." Children handle real words well when they are said with warmth.
- **Be clear about who the parents are.** "Sarah carried you. We are your parents."
- **Avoid words that suggest she gave you away.** She was not giving up her baby. She was helping you have yours.
- **Speak positively about her,** even if your relationship has changed since the birth.
- **Do not overload them.** Answer the question they asked, then stop and let them ask more.

## Books and keepsakes

- **Picture books.** There are children's books written about surrogacy, egg donation and different kinds of families, including ones for two dads, two moms and single parents. Ask your agency or clinic for current recommendations, and read them first to make sure they fit your story.
- **A homemade storybook.** Many families make their own with photos of the ultrasounds, the surrogate, the hospital and the first day home. Children often love this more than any store-bought book.
- **Letters.** Some surrogates write a letter to the baby. Keep it somewhere safe for when your child is old enough.
- **A memory box.** Hospital bracelet, the first scan photo, cards from the surrogate's family.

## What about the surrogate's role over time?

Some families stay close to their surrogate for years, with birthday messages and visits. Others have a lighter connection. Either can be healthy. What matters is that you are honest with your child about who she is, and that you talk about the relationship before the birth so expectations match.
