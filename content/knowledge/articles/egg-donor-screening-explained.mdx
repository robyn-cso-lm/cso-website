Screening is the part of egg donation that happens before anyone starts injections. It is where the clinic, the agency and the donor all make sure donation is safe and right for her, and that intended parents have the information they need.

For donors, screening can feel like a lot of appointments and a lot of questions. For intended parents, it can feel like a black box. So let me open the box a little.

I was an egg donor five times and I founded Little Miracles in 2007. I have been through screening myself and I have walked hundreds of donors and families through it.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Your fertility clinic decides exactly which tests are needed, and they are the right people to explain your results.*

## Why screening matters

Screening protects three groups of people:

1. **The donor**, by making sure donation is medically and emotionally safe for her
2. **The intended parents and future child**, by checking for infections and genetic conditions that could matter
3. **Everyone involved**, by making sure expectations are clear before anyone commits

It is not about finding a "perfect" person. Nobody is perfect. It is about making informed, safe decisions.

## Step one: the application and history

Before any testing, donors share a detailed history. This usually covers:

- Personal medical history
- Family medical history, often going back to grandparents
- Reproductive history, including past pregnancies or donations
- Lifestyle, like smoking, alcohol and medications
- Education, interests and a personal statement

Honesty here is everything. If you are a donor and you are not sure about something in your family history, it is okay to say "I do not know." That is much better than guessing.

## Step two: medical screening

Medical screening is done by the fertility clinic. It commonly includes:

- **A physical exam and review of your history** with a doctor or nurse
- **Bloodwork** to check general health and hormone levels
- **An ultrasound** to look at your ovaries and get a sense of how they may respond to medication
- **Infectious disease testing**, which in Canada follows federal safety rules for donor cells

Some of these tests may need to be done again closer to your cycle. That is normal and is part of the safety rules, not a sign that anything is wrong.

If something comes back unexpected, the clinic will talk with you about what it means. Sometimes it means a short delay or a retest. Sometimes it means donation is not the right fit, and that is never a judgment on you as a person.

## Step three: genetic screening

Genetic screening often has two parts.

**Family history review.** A genetic counsellor or the clinic may look at your family history for conditions that could be inherited.

**Carrier screening.** This is a blood or saliva test that looks for whether you carry a gene for certain conditions. Being a carrier usually means you are healthy and would never know without testing. It matters because if the sperm provider carries a gene for the same condition, there could be a risk for the child.

For intended parents, this is why your clinic may want to compare the donor's carrier results with the sperm provider's. A carrier result in a donor is not automatically a "no." It is information to look at together.

For donors, carrier results can sometimes be useful for your own future family planning too. If something comes up, ask the clinic or a genetic counsellor what it means for you.

## Step four: psychological screening

In Canada, donors meet with a mental health professional who understands third party reproduction. This is not a test you pass or fail by being "happy enough." It is a conversation about:

- Why you want to donate
- How you feel about a child being born from your donation
- Your support system
- How you feel about future contact, if the child wants it
- How you would cope if things do not go as planned

Many donors tell me this conversation was actually one of the most meaningful parts of the process. It is your chance to think things through out loud with someone whose job is to look out for you.

Intended parents usually have their own counselling session too. You can read more about the emotional side for intended parents in [Coming to Terms With Using an Egg Donor](/knowledge-centre/coming-to-terms-with-using-an-egg-donor).

## Step five: legal

Before medications start, the donor and intended parents each get independent legal advice and sign an ovum donation agreement. That is not "screening" exactly, but it is part of the same "are we all ready?" checkpoint. More in [The Ovum Donation Agreement and Independent Legal Advice, Explained](/knowledge-centre/ovum-donation-agreement-canada).

## How long does screening take?

It depends on the clinic, the donor's schedule and how quickly results come back. Some parts happen quickly, others take more time. Your clinic and coordinator can give you a realistic timeline. For the bigger picture, see [How long does egg donation take?](/knowledge-centre/faqs/how-long-does-egg-donation-take)

## Questions intended parents can ask

- What tests has this donor completed, and what is still pending?
- When were the infectious disease tests done, and will any be repeated?
- What does the carrier screening cover, and how does it compare with ours?
- Has the donor met with a counsellor?
- Is she open to future contact?

## Questions donors can ask

- Which appointments do I need, and how much time will each take?
- Will I get copies of my results?
- If something unexpected comes up, who will explain it to me?
- What expenses can be reimbursed during screening? (See [Egg Donor Expenses in Canada](/knowledge-centre/egg-donor-expenses-what-can-be-reimbursed).)

## A word to donors who do not get through screening

Sometimes screening shows that donation is not the right path. I know that can sting, especially if you came in with a big, generous heart. Please know that the screening exists to protect you just as much as anyone else, and your desire to help is a beautiful thing no matter what.
