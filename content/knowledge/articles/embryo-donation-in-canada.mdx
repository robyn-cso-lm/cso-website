There are two kinds of people who end up reading about embryo donation.

The first family has finished building their family through IVF. Their children are here, and there are embryos still sitting in a freezer. Every year the storage bill arrives, and every year someone asks, "What do we do with them?"

The second family is still waiting. Maybe they cannot use their own eggs or sperm, maybe IVF has not worked, or maybe they love the idea of giving an existing embryo a chance at life. For them, a donated embryo might be the path to their baby.

This article is for both of you. I have seen embryo donation create families from both sides of that freezer, and it is one of the most generous and emotionally layered things I see in this work.

*This article is general information, not legal or medical advice. Your clinic and your lawyer will confirm what applies to you.*

## What embryo donation is

Embryo donation is when people who created embryos through IVF give some or all of their unused embryos to another person or couple, so that person can try to have a baby. The embryos are usually frozen. The recipient (or a gestational surrogate) has a frozen embryo transfer, and if it works, the baby is genetically related to the donors, not to the recipients.

People sometimes call it "embryo adoption." In Canada, it is not a legal adoption. It is assisted reproduction, governed by federal and provincial law the same way other donor conception is.

## The law in Canada

### No buying or selling

Under the Assisted Human Reproduction Act, it is a crime to buy or sell an in vitro embryo, or to offer to. Embryo donation in Canada has to be truly a donation. Donors can be reimbursed for receipted expenses directly related to the donation, in line with the federal reimbursement regulations, but they cannot be paid for the embryos themselves.

### Consent is everything

Federal consent rules under the AHRA require written consent from the people whose embryos they are (the "donors") before an embryo can be used for someone else's reproduction. A few points that often surprise people:

- **If the embryos were created by a couple, both people usually have to consent.** If the couple has separated, that can get complicated.
- **If donor eggs or donor sperm were used to create the embryos,** the original gamete donor's consent may also matter. The original donor agreement may say whether onward donation is allowed. This is something to check early.
- **Consent can generally be withdrawn** up until the embryo is used. Your lawyer will explain exactly when that point is.

### Health Canada safety rules

Because the embryos were made from eggs and sperm, Health Canada's Safety of Sperm and Ova Regulations come into play. Embryos that were originally created for a couple's own use did not always go through donor-level screening and testing. Before donation, clinics generally need to look at what screening was done, and the donors may be asked for additional history and blood tests. Where some requirements cannot be met, there is a pathway called exceptional distribution that may allow use with the recipient's informed consent. Your clinic will explain what applies.

### Parentage

Legal parentage is decided by provincial law. In general, the people who donate embryos are not the legal parents of a child born from them, and the intended parents are. But the details depend on your province, especially if a surrogate is involved. See [parentage and birth registration after surrogacy](/knowledge-centre/parentage-and-birth-registration-after-surrogacy).

## Legal agreements and ILA

Most embryo donations involve a written agreement between the donating family and the receiving family, with each side getting independent legal advice (ILA) from its own lawyer. A good agreement usually covers:

- That the donors give up any parental rights and responsibilities
- That the recipients accept full legal and financial responsibility
- What happens to any embryos the recipients do not use (returned, donated onward, given to research, or discarded)
- Openness: whether identifying information is shared, whether there will be contact, and what the child will be told
- Whether the recipients may use the embryos with a surrogate
- Confidentiality and medical information sharing

The same principles apply as in [the ovum donation agreement and independent legal advice](/knowledge-centre/ovum-donation-agreement-canada).

## Counselling: for both families

Most clinics require, or strongly recommend, implications counselling for both the donors and the recipients. This is not a test you pass or fail. It is a chance to think through things like:

- For donors: how will you feel knowing a full genetic sibling of your children is being raised in another family? What will you tell your own children?
- For recipients: how do you feel about carrying or raising a baby with no genetic link to you? How and when will you tell your child?
- For both: what level of openness feels right, now and in 18 years?

These are big questions. A good counsellor helps everyone make choices they can live with long term.

## How matching generally works

There is no single national embryo donation system in Canada. Matching tends to happen in a few ways:

- **Through a fertility clinic.** Some clinics run their own embryo donation programs, matching patients who have embryos to donate with patients who want to receive them.
- **Directed (known) donation.** A donating family chooses a specific recipient they know, such as a friend, relative, or someone they connected with through a community. This still goes through a clinic and lawyers.
- **Through agencies or matching services.** Some organizations help connect donating and receiving families.

Waiting times, eligibility and how much information you receive vary a lot. Ask your clinic what it offers and what the process looks like.

## What receiving families should ask

- What do we know about the donors' medical, family and genetic history?
- Were the embryos genetically tested (for example, PGT-A)? See [PGT-A embryo testing explained](/knowledge-centre/pgt-a-embryo-testing-explained).
- How were the embryos graded and frozen, and when? See [embryo grading explained](/knowledge-centre/embryo-grading-explained).
- How many embryos are being donated, and are there full genetic siblings already born?
- Will the embryos need to be shipped to our clinic? See [shipping embryos and gametes in Canada](/knowledge-centre/shipping-embryos-and-gametes-canada).
- What openness or contact did the donors agree to?

## What donating families should ask

- Does our original consent or donor agreement allow us to donate?
- If we used donor eggs or sperm, do we need that donor's consent?
- Can we choose or meet the recipients?
- What will happen to embryos the recipients do not use?
- Would we want to be contacted if a child is born, or when that child is an adult?

If you are not sure yet whether donation is right for you, read [what to do with leftover embryos](/knowledge-centre/what-to-do-with-leftover-embryos). It lays out every option, gently.

## Embryo donation and surrogacy

Donated embryos can be transferred to a gestational surrogate when the recipient cannot carry a pregnancy. That combination needs extra care, because the surrogacy agreement and the embryo donation agreement have to fit together, and everyone's consents need to line up.
